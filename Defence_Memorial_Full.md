**STATEMENT OF JURISDICTION**

The Defence for General Roger Thorn respectfully appears before Pre-Trial Chamber I in the Situation in the Republic of Pallavon. On 20 May 2026, the Chamber issued a summons to appear pursuant to Article 58(7) of the Rome Statute, and General Thorn voluntarily travelled to The Hague and made his initial appearance on 15 June 2026 pursuant to Article 60(1) of the Rome Statute and Rule 121(1) of the Rules of Procedure and Evidence. The present hearing is held pursuant to Article 61 of the Rome Statute. In accordance with Instruction 4 of the Moot Problem, the Defence addresses conflict classification; no other issue of jurisdiction or admissibility is raised.

**IDENTIFICATION OF ISSUES**

**I.** Whether the Prosecution has established substantial grounds to believe that General Thorn is individually criminally responsible under Article 25(3)(c) of the Rome Statute for aiding, abetting or otherwise assisting in the war crime of torture or inhuman treatment, including biological experiments, under Article 8(2)(a)(ii), in respect of the Jaku treatment administered to Titan soldiers between 2 and 10 April 2025.

**II.** Whether the Prosecution has established substantial grounds to believe that General Thorn is individually criminally responsible under Article 25(3)(b) of the Rome Statute for ordering, soliciting or inducing the war crime of intentionally directing attacks against civilian objects under Article 8(2)(b)(ii), in respect of the missile strike on three schools on 22 July 2025.

**STATEMENT OF FACTS**

- **Operation Titan and the illness.** On 10 March 2025, General Thorn led a platoon of Damaran soldiers into the Damaran Gap. Disease halted the mission and five soldiers died. On 19 March, Thorn, himself ill, sent the five healthiest soldiers onward and withdrew the rest to Damaroi, setting fire to the Pallavon bank of the River Targa as they left. The fire was reported as a natural fire and was never investigated. [SAF 11–14]
- **The treatment.** All 40 returning soldiers contracted an unknown disease with severe, typhoid-like symptoms, and four were placed in induced comas. Conventional treatment was failing: ‘[s]ome of the guys are holding on for their lives … their conditions aren't getting better with the regular stuff’. Dr Nora Loff, head scientist of Damaroi's leading biomedical institute, advised that the drug Jaku ‘could help restore their health’ and was ‘ready for human trials’. Thorn replied: ‘if the Doc says it's good to go, then it can't be wrong’. [SAF 15–17] From 2 April, Dr Loff administered Jaku to every platoon member, including Thorn himself. The conscious soldiers verbally agreed; the four comatose soldiers woke within three days. Dr Loff assured Thorn that the side-effects reported by some soldiers were temporary and recommended a higher dose, which he approved. By 10 April, all 40 soldiers had regained full health, and the injections stopped. [SAF 18–20] The five soldiers who had continued into Pallavon had, as was later established, died in early April. [SAF 53]
- **The schools.** Hostilities began in June 2025, and Pallavon ordered mass conscription, registering over two million recruits and receiving weapons and trainers from Bhumara by rail. [SAF 24–29] On 5 July, Pallavon's Cabinet decided to repurpose every school larger than two hectares and within one kilometre of the railway ‘as new sites to train recruits and prepare operations’. Twelve of the 20 qualifying schools were converted at once. After Damaroi struck them, five more were evacuated and converted, each strike following confirmation of conversion. [SAF 37–41] On 20 July, the remaining three qualifying schools were dismissed from teaching. Having observed the pattern of conversions, Thorn gave the order to target the remaining qualifying schools and provided strike coordinates. President Veyar asked, ‘Are you sure we should strike them?’. Thorn replied that ‘every school of that size is being converted’, that the grounds had been evacuated, and that the operation should take place at night ‘to minimise risk of harming civilians’. At 1:10 am on 22 July, missiles were launched from Como City. Most were intercepted; two schools were partially damaged and the third destroyed, killing cleaning staff employed there. [SAF 42–43]

**SUMMARY OF PLEADINGS**

**Count 1.** Jaku was an emergency treatment for dying soldiers, not a biological experiment. It was given to every patient, including Thorn himself, without any research design, and it stopped once the patients recovered. Measured against a life-threatening illness, a treatment that restored full health did not seriously endanger it, and its passing side-effects fall far short of inhuman treatment. The treatment was therapeutic and medically justified, and emergency treatment without consent is permitted by legal systems across the world. The Titans were not protected persons: the Elements, the Geneva system, ICTY jurisprudence, national courts and the Medical Trial all confine the grave breaches of Article 8(2)(a) to victims belonging to an adverse party. No international armed conflict existed between 2 and 10 April 2025, and the medical treatment of Damaran soldiers in Damaroi had no nexus to any conflict. Thorn, who deferred to a medical expert, did not act for the purpose of facilitating a crime and lacked the required knowledge.

**Count 2.** The three schools were military objectives by purpose. A formal Cabinet decision, its systematic implementation at 17 of 20 qualifying sites, the evacuation of the final three, and mass mobilisation established their intended military use, as *Hirgigo* recognises. Denying Pallavon its last replacement training sites offered a definite military advantage. Article 52(3) addresses doubt about present use, not established purpose, and the non-binding Safe Schools Guidelines cannot change that. Thorn believed, on substantial evidence, that the schools were military objectives; he was therefore not virtually certain that he was attacking civilian objects, and any error, whether of fact or about a normative element, negates intent. At most, the case raises the uncharged question of proportionality. Finally, the word ‘order’ in the agreed facts does not establish ordering under Article 25(3)(b): the Prosecution has not shown Thorn's authority over those who launched the missiles, nor that his assessment, rather than the President's own decision, caused the strike.

### A. GENERAL THORN IS NOT INDIVIDUALLY CRIMINALLY RESPONSIBLE UNDER ARTICLE 25(3)(c) FOR THE WAR CRIME OF TORTURE OR INHUMAN TREATMENT, INCLUDING BIOLOGICAL EXPERIMENTS, UNDER ARTICLE 8(2)(a)(ii)


#### 1. Jaku was an emergency treatment, not a biological experiment

A biological experiment is conduct whose *primary purpose* is to study the as yet unknown effects of a product on the human body.¹ The prohibition therefore turns on purpose and design, not on the novelty of the substance. It does not prevent doctors from trying new therapeutic methods dictated by the wish to improve the patient's condition,² and the drafters intended precisely to preserve that freedom.³ The post-war cases show what a biological experiment looks like: prisoners in the enemy's power, deliberately infected to test vaccines, by doctors who took no risk themselves.⁴ Jaku bears none of these features.

Jaku was given to dying soldiers because conventional treatment had failed. Every patient received it, and so did the commanding officer himself. There was no control group, no selection of subjects and no research protocol, and the injections stopped as soon as the patients recovered.⁵ That is the design of treatment, not of a study. Dr Loff's further hopes for the drug, such as immunity, strength and national prestige, do not change its primary purpose, because a programme may pursue more than one goal without losing its genuine character.⁶ Nor does Thorn's approval of a higher dose after feeling ‘newfound strength himself’ prove experimentation; it shows only that he had personally experienced the drug working. A commander who takes the drug himself is not using his soldiers as guinea-pigs.⁷ The Prosecution has offered no ‘concrete and tangible proof’ that Jaku's primary purpose was to study its effects.⁸


#### 2. The treatment did not seriously endanger the Titans' health

Whether an intervention seriously endangers health is judged by its foreseeable consequences ‘having regard to the state of health of the person’.⁹ For soldiers in comas or ‘holding on for their lives’, the danger lay in the illness, not in the cure. Jaku woke the four comatose soldiers within three days and restored all 40 to full health within eight. Headaches subsided, and the remaining disorientation and paranoia were ‘sporadic’ and, on the medical advice received, ‘temporary’.¹⁰ The paranoia reported in July took the form of concern that the Titans' covert mission had been exposed, a concern with an obvious operational explanation, and the fatigue was reported three months after the charged period.¹¹

Nor can the Prosecution fall back on inhuman treatment. That crime requires severe physical or mental pain or suffering,¹² and passing side-effects of a successful life-saving treatment do not reach that threshold.¹³ The stricter regime of Additional Protocol I is equally unavailable, because it protects only persons ‘in the power of the adverse Party’ or otherwise deprived of liberty;¹⁴ soldiers treated by their own army's doctors are neither.


#### 3. The treatment was therapeutic, medically justified and in the Titans' interest

*Salus aegroti suprema lex*: the welfare of the patient is the supreme law of medicine. Where proven treatments ‘have been ineffective’, a physician may use an unproven intervention that offers hope of ‘saving life, re-establishing health or alleviating suffering’,¹⁵ and a measure that is a therapeutic necessity cannot, as a general rule, be regarded as inhuman or degrading.¹⁶ A doctor who acts in accordance with a responsible body of medical opinion acts properly.¹⁷ Jaku met every part of that test. Regular treatment had failed, the soldiers were dying, and the head of Damaroi's leading biomedical institute advised that Jaku could restore their health, which it did; the dose was adjusted on her advice and stopped once recovery was complete.¹⁸ The preservation of life in an emergency is the paramount duty of every doctor.¹⁹ Trying a promising treatment when all else had failed discharged that duty; it did not depart from it.

Consent confirms the therapeutic character. The Elements of Article 8(2)(a)(ii), unlike those of Article 8(2)(b)(x), do not exclude consent,²⁰ and that silence must be resolved in the accused's favour.²¹ *Volenti non fit injuria*: the conscious soldiers agreed to the treatment.²² For the four comatose soldiers, legal systems across the world permit necessary treatment without consent where the patient cannot consent in an emergency: England on grounds of necessity and best interests,²³ Canada under the emergency doctrine,²⁴ India²⁵ and the United States.²⁶ Even where an intervention is classified as research, United States and European Union law permit it without prior consent in an emergency.²⁷ That uniform practice is a general principle of law.²⁸ For the conscious soldiers, adjusting the dose within a course of treatment they had already accepted required no fresh consent; for the comatose soldiers, the same emergency principle continued to apply.²⁹ In the further alternative, and without conceding that any element of the crime is established, Thorn's initial approval was necessary and reasonable to avoid the imminent threat of death to his soldiers, a threat beyond his control, and he intended no greater harm than the one he sought to avoid.³⁰


#### 4. The Titans were not protected persons for the purposes of Article 8(2)(a)

Article 8(2)(a) criminalises grave breaches against persons ‘protected under the provisions of the relevant Geneva Convention’.³¹ The Elements identify those persons. The perpetrator ‘needs only to know that the victim belonged to an adverse party to the conflict’, and ‘[t]his footnote also applies to the corresponding element in each crime under article 8(2)(a)’.³² The drafters of every grave breach therefore assumed an enemy victim. The treaties must be read in that context and with the rest of the Geneva system,³³ which ties protected status to falling into the power of the other side,³⁴ and the ICTY Appeals Chamber has consistently defined protected status by allegiance to the opposing party.³⁵ Scholarship confirms that the law of armed conflict does not, as a rule, protect a party's own forces against their own State.³⁶ National courts agree: the Privy Council, on appeal from Malaysia, held that nationals of the detaining State could not claim prisoner-of-war protection against that State.³⁷ So does the first precedent on medical experiments: in the Medical Trial, experiments on German nationals were punished as crimes against humanity, and only those on prisoners of war and civilians of occupied territories were war crimes.³⁸

The *Ntaganda* appeal decision does not assist the Prosecution. It concerned rape and sexual slavery under Articles 8(2)(b)(xxii) and 8(2)(e)(vi), which carry no protected-status element, and it expressly contrasted them with the grave breaches of Article 8(2)(a), which do.³⁹ Its remarks on the First Geneva Convention were unnecessary to its decision. *Nullum crimen sine lege stricta*: the definition of a crime ‘shall be strictly construed’ and, *in dubio pro reo*, any ambiguity resolved in favour of the accused.⁴⁰ The Titans were Damaroi's own soldiers treated by Damaroi's own medical service. Thorn knew that they belonged to his own side, not to an adverse party, so the awareness required by Element 5 is also absent.⁴¹


#### 5. No international armed conflict existed between 2 and 10 April 2025, and the treatment had no nexus to any conflict

An IAC requires ‘armed hostilities between States through their respective armed forces’.⁴² However low the threshold, some resort to armed force *against* another State is required,⁴³ and State practice supports a minimum level of hostilities.⁴⁴ Just as the International Court of Justice distinguished an armed attack from a ‘mere frontier incident’, not every movement of soldiers across a border is a use of armed force.⁴⁵ Before 2 April, there had been no hostilities at all. The Titans engaged no Pallavon forces or civilians and turned back because of disease.⁴⁶ The fire Thorn set on an uninhabited riverbank was treated by Pallavon itself as a natural event and never investigated.⁴⁷ Neither State acted as though it were at war; the first armed exchange occurred at Santo in June.⁴⁸

Even if an IAC existed, the treatment had no nexus to it. The conflict must have played a substantial part in the perpetrator's ability to commit the crime, his decision to commit it, the manner of its commission or its purpose;⁴⁹ a relevant factor is whether the victim belonged to the opposing party.⁵⁰ The treatment of Damaroi's sick soldiers in a Damaran hospital by a Damaran doctor is exactly what would have occurred in peacetime. The victims were not enemy nationals, and the only Titans still in Pallavon were five soldiers who, forensic evidence shows, died of the disease in early April without ever engaging a Pallavon force.⁵¹ Thorn's remark that the Titans ‘need to be stronger’ was made before Jaku was ever proposed; it expressed general resolve, not a plan to experiment.⁵²


#### 6. Thorn did not aid, abet or otherwise assist the crime with the required purpose and knowledge

Accessory liability presupposes a principal crime. The principal, Dr Loff, administered Jaku believing that it ‘could help restore their health’,⁵³ so the Prosecution has not shown the non-therapeutic intent on which Thorn's liability depends. Article 25(3)(c) further requires that the accessory act ‘for the purpose of facilitating’ the crime, a subjective threshold that goes beyond Article 30.⁵⁴ The phrase derives from national law, where the accomplice must ‘in some sort associate himself with the venture’ and have advance knowledge of the whole crime.⁵⁵ The assistance must also have a substantial effect on the crime, and international courts have required that it be directed to the criminal, rather than the lawful, aspects of the principal's activity.⁵⁶

Thorn's purpose was to save his soldiers, not to facilitate an experiment. His words show deference to medical judgement: ‘if the Doc says it's good to go, then it can't be wrong … if you think it'll help, I say we give it to the whole group’. The drug was obtained, prescribed and administered by others.⁵⁷ He also lacked the knowledge, required by Article 30(3), that the treatment was non-therapeutic;⁵⁸ recklessness and *dolus eventualis* do not suffice.⁵⁹ Milch was acquitted because the evidence did not show that he knew of the experiments' criminal character.⁶⁰ Thorn is in a stronger position still, having relied on an expert's assurance that the treatment would help and that its side-effects were temporary. Any error was one of fact, and it negates the mental element.⁶¹ *Actus non facit reum nisi mens sit rea*. Count 1 should not be confirmed.


### B. GENERAL THORN IS NOT INDIVIDUALLY CRIMINALLY RESPONSIBLE UNDER ARTICLE 25(3)(b) FOR THE WAR CRIME OF INTENTIONALLY DIRECTING ATTACKS AGAINST CIVILIAN OBJECTS UNDER ARTICLE 8(2)(b)(ii)


#### 1. The three schools were military objectives by purpose

An object is a military objective if, by its nature, location, *purpose* or use, it makes an effective contribution to military action and its destruction offers a definite military advantage; this definition is customary.⁶² *Ut res magis valeat quam pereat*: ‘purpose’ must be given its own meaning, for if it merely repeated ‘use’ it would be surplusage.⁶³ It ‘is concerned with the intended future use of an object, while that of “use” is concerned with its present function’.⁶⁴ In *Hirgigo*, the Eritrea–Ethiopia Claims Commission agreed that purpose ‘means the future intended use of an object’, and held that a power station still under construction, not yet producing power, was a military objective because of its intended contribution.⁶⁵ The Prosecution's central fact, that ‘no positive action had yet been taken to use the premises’, therefore concerns use, not purpose.⁶⁶

The schools' military purpose was concrete, not speculative. First, Pallavon's Cabinet formally decided to repurpose every school larger than two hectares and within one kilometre of the railway ‘as new sites to train recruits and prepare operations’. Secondly, that decision was implemented at 17 of the 20 qualifying sites, in a documented cycle in which each destroyed facility was replaced by the next qualifying school. Thirdly, the final three schools had been dismissed from teaching and their grounds evacuated, the same first step that had preceded every earlier conversion. Fourthly, Pallavon was mobilising over two million recruits, with weapons and trainers arriving by rail.⁶⁷ These indicators were specific to the objects attacked,⁶⁸ and they supply precisely the specification that President van Houtte found lacking in *Hirgigo*.⁶⁹ The risk to those schools was, moreover, of Pallavon's own making. A defending State must avoid placing military objectives within or near densely populated areas;⁷⁰ having adopted a programme to convert its schools into military sites, Pallavon cannot invoke their former character to shield them.

Their destruction offered a definite military advantage. The advantage must be concrete and perceptible rather than potential,⁷¹ and it is assessed in the context of the military operations as a whole.⁷² Destroying the last three qualifying sites denied Pallavon the capacity to reconstitute its destroyed training infrastructure in the middle of a mass mobilisation, a capacity it had exercised five times in a single week.⁷³ Like the power plant in *Hirgigo*, the schools were intended to replace capacity already lost, and denying that replacement was a distinct military advantage.⁷⁴


#### 2. Article 52(3) does not displace the evidence of military purpose

The presumption in Article 52(3) applies where there is doubt whether an object ‘*is being used*’ to make an effective contribution to military action.⁷⁵ *Expressio unius est exclusio alterius*: the provision names present use and says nothing of purpose. Reading it to cancel the separate purpose limb of Article 52(2) would deprive that limb of effect,⁷⁶ and would force a belligerent to wait until a facility is operational, a result inconsistent with *Hirgigo*, where a facility not yet operational was held to be a military objective.⁷⁷ The Defence accepts that schools are ordinarily civilian. But President Veyar's doubt concerned only whether the schools were ‘actually being used’; Thorn's answer addressed their purpose: ‘Even if they are not being used now, their purpose is clear’.⁷⁸ On purpose, the Cabinet decision, the conversion cycle and the evacuations left no genuine doubt.

The Safe Schools Guidelines do not alter this. They are expressly non-binding and without prejudice to existing international law,⁷⁹ and they cannot amend the definition of a military objective agreed by States in Article 52(2) and recognised as customary.


#### 3. Thorn did not intend to attack civilian objects

Element 3 requires that the perpetrator ‘intended such *civilian objects* to be the object of the attack’.⁸⁰ The question is therefore not whether Thorn meant the missiles to strike the schools, but whether he meant to attack them *as civilian objects*. War crimes require knowledge of the facts that make the conduct criminal, and an honest mistake about those facts is a defence.⁸¹ Targeting decisions are judged on the information reasonably available at the time, not with hindsight.⁸² That standard, first applied in *Rendulic*, has passed into State practice:⁸³ a commander who honestly concludes on the information before him that an object is a military objective commits no crime, even if later shown to be wrong.⁸⁴ Findings of unlawful attack cannot rest on standards constructed after the event,⁸⁵ and in the Kunduz airstrike, a commander who acted on intelligence he believed reliable was found to lack criminal intent.⁸⁶ Thorn likewise acted on the Cabinet programme, 17 documented conversions, the replacement pattern and the evacuations when he concluded that ‘every school of that size is being converted’.⁸⁷

Article 30 requires that Thorn meant to attack civilian objects or was aware that he would do so ‘in the ordinary course of events’.⁸⁸ That standard means virtual certainty, not a possibility or a risk;⁸⁹ recklessness and *dolus eventualis* are excluded.⁹⁰ A commander who believes a site is about to become a military training facility is not virtually certain that he is attacking a civilian object. At most, Thorn was mistaken about whether Pallavon would imminently convert these particular schools. *Ignorantia facti excusat*: that is a mistake of fact, and it negates the mental element.⁹¹ Even if the Chamber regarded his error as one about the legal characterisation of the schools as military objectives, a mistake about a normative element of the crime equally negates intent.⁹²

Thorn's own conduct confirms the absence of any intent to attack civilian objects. He recommended a night strike ‘to minimise risk of harming civilians’, the very precaution that IHL requires in attacks on military objectives.⁹³ If the Prosecution's real complaint is the civilian harm at the third school, that harm arose from an attack on what Thorn believed to be a military objective. It could at most raise a question of excessive incidental harm under Article 8(2)(b)(iv), a crime the Prosecution has not charged.⁹⁴


#### 4. Any shortfall in verification is a question of precautions, not the crime charged

The Prosecution will contrast this strike with the earlier ones, which followed confirmation of each conversion.⁹⁵ The comparison does not prove the crime charged. Article 8(2)(b)(ii) punishes intentionally directing an attack against civilian objects; it does not criminalise a failure to verify a target. The duty to verify arises under Additional Protocol I,⁹⁶ but the Rome Statute contains no corresponding war crime, and even Additional Protocol I treats as grave breaches only the wilful attacks it lists, not omissions in precaution.⁹⁷ *Nullum crimen sine lege*: a duty of conduct under IHL cannot be turned into a crime of intentional attack by relabelling it.⁹⁸ At most, a claim that Thorn should have waited longer alleges negligence, and negligence is not intent.⁹⁹

In any event, the duty is to do everything *feasible* to verify, meaning what is practicable or practically possible in the circumstances ruling at the time, taking account of humanitarian and military considerations.¹⁰⁰ *Lex non cogit ad impossibilia*: the law does not demand certainty, but a reasonable judgement on the information available.¹⁰¹ Thorn met that standard. The Titans had monitored the converted schools across the country, supplying over 300 images and videos, delivery logs of weapons and uniforms, and records of night-time activity.¹⁰² They had observed that every conversion began with the school's suspension and evacuation, and that step had now occurred at all three schools.¹⁰³ The President raised his doubt and Thorn answered it on that evidence; a commander who weighs a doubt and resolves it on the information before him is verifying, not ignoring, the question.¹⁰⁴ Waiting for further confirmation would have meant waiting until the schools were filled with recruits and weapons. Thorn instead proposed striking evacuated premises at night, the course least likely to harm anyone.¹⁰⁵ That is a precaution taken, not one ignored.


#### 5. Thorn did not order, solicit or induce the attack

Ordering requires a person in a position of authority who uses that authority to instruct another to commit the crime.¹⁰⁶ The agreed facts' use of the word ‘order’ does not settle the question. The label a narrative gives to a communication is not a finding that the elements of Article 25(3)(b) are met,¹⁰⁷ and ordering requires proof that the accused used authority over the person who carried out the crime.¹⁰⁸ Authority over the recipient must at least be reasonably implied from the evidence, and the order must have a direct effect on the commission of the crime.¹⁰⁹ Ordering is not advice; it means using a subordinate to carry out the crime.¹¹⁰ *Affirmanti incumbit probatio*, yet the Prosecution identifies no recipient. The record does not show who launched the missiles, who authorised the launch, what chain of command linked Thorn to the missile force in Como City, or that anyone there was obliged to act on his word.¹¹¹ It shows instead that Thorn's formal role was to supply intelligence and planning. The President tasked him to be Damaroi's ‘eyes and ears’ and to supply ‘sound intel and a plan’ for a missile campaign the President himself directed,¹¹² and Thorn's earlier contribution took the form of a ‘recommendation’.¹¹³ The President's question, ‘Are you sure we should strike them?’, confirms that Thorn's word was not self-executing and that the decision to launch remained with the President.¹¹⁴ Supplying coordinates is, at most, assistance, a mode not charged; it is not an order.

Nor did Thorn solicit or induce the attack. Inducement requires influence that has a direct effect on the commission of the crime.¹¹⁵ The President was already conducting a missile campaign to sting Pallavon ‘where it hurts’ and had his own reasons to continue it.¹¹⁶ The record does not show that Thorn's assessment, rather than the President's independent decision, caused the launch, or that Thorn influenced any person who carried it out. The Prosecution has not provided concrete and tangible proof of the mode of liability charged, and Count 2 should not be confirmed.


**PRAYER FOR RELIEF**

For the foregoing reasons, the Defence respectfully requests Pre-Trial Chamber I, pursuant to Article 61(7)(b) of the Rome Statute, to:

i. DECLINE to confirm Count 1, in respect of the war crime of torture or inhuman treatment, including biological experiments, under Article 8(2)(a)(ii), charged under Article 25(3)(c);

ii. DECLINE to confirm Count 2, in respect of the war crime of intentionally directing attacks against civilian objects under Article 8(2)(b)(ii), charged under Article 25(3)(b); and

iii. ORDER that General Roger Thorn not be committed to trial.

Respectfully submitted,
Counsel for the Defence
