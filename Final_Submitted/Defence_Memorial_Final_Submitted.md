# **STATEMENT OF JURISDICTION** {#statement-of-jurisdiction .unnumbered}

Counsel for General Roger Thorn \["the Defence"\] respectfully appear
before Pre-Trial Chamber I of the International Criminal Court \["the
Court"\] in the Situation in the Republic of Pallavon. On 20 May 2026,
the Chamber issued a summons to appear pursuant to Article 58(7) of the
Rome Statute of the International Criminal Court \["the Rome Statute"\].
On 1 June 2026, General Thorn voluntarily travelled to The Hague, and he
made his initial appearance on 15 June 2026 pursuant to Article 60(1) of
the Rome Statute and Rule 121(1) of the Rules of Procedure and Evidence.
The present hearing is held pursuant to Article 61 of the Rome Statute.
The Defence submits that, because no international armed conflict
existed between 2 and 10 April 2025, the conduct charged in Count 1
falls outside Article 8(2)(a) of the Rome Statute. That submission is
developed in the Pleadings. In all other respects, the Defence does not
contest the jurisdiction of the Court.

# **IDENTIFICATION OF ISSUES** {#identification-of-issues .unnumbered}

**I.** Whether the Prosecution has established substantial grounds to
believe that General Thorn is individually criminally responsible under
Article 25(3)(c) of the Rome Statute for aiding, abetting or otherwise
assisting in the war crime of torture or inhuman treatment, including
biological experiments, under Article 8(2)(a)(ii), in respect of the
Jaku treatment administered to Titan soldiers between 2 and 10 April
2025.

**II.** Whether the Prosecution has established substantial grounds to
believe that General Thorn is individually criminally responsible under
Article 25(3)(b) of the Rome Statute for ordering, soliciting or
inducing the war crime of intentionally directing attacks against
civilian objects under Article 8(2)(b)(ii), in respect of the missile
strike on three schools on 22 July 2025.

# **STATEMENT OF FACTS** {#statement-of-facts .unnumbered}

In March 2025, disease halted a mission into the Damaran Gap led by
General Roger Thorn, Chief of the Damaran Defence Force, and five
soldiers died. On 19 March, Thorn, himself ill, sent the five healthiest
soldiers onward and withdrew the rest, setting fire to the Pallavon bank
of the River Targa. The fire was reported as natural and never
investigated. The 40 returning soldiers fell severely ill with an
unknown, typhoid-like disease, and four were placed in induced comas.
With conventional treatment failing and some soldiers 'holding on for
their lives', Dr Nora Loff, head scientist of Damaroi's leading
biomedical institute, advised that Jaku 'could help restore their
health' and was 'ready for human trials'. Thorn replied that 'if the Doc
says it's good to go, then it can't be wrong'. From 2 April, Dr Loff
gave Jaku to every platoon member, including Thorn. The conscious
soldiers verbally agreed, and the four comatose soldiers woke within
three days. Assured by Dr Loff that some soldiers' side-effects were
temporary, Thorn approved the higher dose she recommended. By 10 April,
all 40 had regained full health and the injections stopped. After the
first armed confrontation at Santo in June 2025, Pallavon ordered mass
conscription, registering over two million recruits and receiving
weapons and trainers from Bhumara by rail. On 5 July, its Cabinet
decided to repurpose schools larger than two hectares and within one
kilometre of the railway 'as new sites to train recruits and prepare
operations'. Twelve of the 20 qualifying schools were converted at once
and struck on Thorn's recommendation on 13--14 July. Five more were
evacuated and converted, and each was struck once conversion was
confirmed. On 20 July, the last three were dismissed from teaching,
although no positive action had yet been taken to use them for military
purposes. Having observed this pattern, Thorn ordered them targeted and
provided strike coordinates. When President Veyar asked, 'Are you sure
we should strike them?', Thorn replied that 'every school of that size
is being converted', that the grounds had been evacuated, and that a
night strike would 'minimise risk of harming civilians'. At 1:10 am on
22 July, missiles were launched from Como City. Most were intercepted,
but two schools were partially damaged and the third was destroyed,
killing cleaning staff employed by the school.

# **SUMMARY OF PLEADINGS** {#summary-of-pleadings .unnumbered}

## **Count 1: Thorn did not aid or abet any biological experiment** {#count-1-thorn-did-not-aid-or-abet-any-biological-experiment .unnumbered}

Jaku was an emergency treatment for dying soldiers, not a biological
experiment. It was given to every patient, including Thorn himself,
without any research design, and it stopped once the patients recovered.
Measured against a life-threatening illness, a treatment that restored
full health did not seriously endanger it, and its passing side-effects
fall far short of inhuman treatment. The treatment was therapeutic and
medically justified, and emergency treatment without consent is
permitted by legal systems across the world. The Titans were not
protected persons: the Elements, the Geneva system, ICTY jurisprudence,
national courts and the Medical Trial all confine the grave breaches of
Article 8(2)(a) to victims belonging to an adverse party. No
international armed conflict existed between 2 and 10 April 2025, and
the medical treatment of Damaran soldiers in Damaroi had no nexus to any
conflict. Thorn, who deferred to a medical expert, did not act for the
purpose of facilitating a crime and lacked the required knowledge.

## **Count 2: Thorn did not order an attack on civilian objects** {#count-2-thorn-did-not-order-an-attack-on-civilian-objects .unnumbered}

The three schools were military objectives by purpose. A formal Cabinet
decision, its systematic implementation at 17 of 20 qualifying sites,
the evacuation of the final three, and mass mobilisation established
their intended military use, as *Hirgigo* recognises. Denying Pallavon
its last replacement training sites offered a definite military
advantage. Article 52(3) addresses doubt about present use, not
established purpose. The absence of positive action at the final three
schools likewise concerns use, not purpose, and the non-binding Safe
Schools Guidelines cannot change that. Thorn believed, on substantial
evidence, that the schools were military objectives. He was therefore
not virtually certain that he was attacking civilian objects, and any
error, whether of fact or about a normative element, negates intent. The
record does not identify who launched the missiles or what they knew.
Any shortfall in verifying the targets concerns precautions, not the
crime charged, and Thorn did what was feasible on the information before
him. At most, the case raises the uncharged question of proportionality.
Finally, the word 'order' in the agreed facts does not establish
ordering under Article 25(3)(b). The Prosecution has not shown Thorn's
authority over those who launched the missiles, nor that his assessment,
rather than the President's own decision, caused the strike.

# **PLEADINGS** {#pleadings .unnumbered}

## **GENERAL THORN IS NOT INDIVIDUALLY CRIMINALLY RESPONSIBLE UNDER ARTICLE 25(3)(c) FOR THE WAR CRIME OF TORTURE OR INHUMAN TREATMENT, INCLUDING BIOLOGICAL EXPERIMENTS, UNDER ARTICLE 8(2)(a)(ii)**

The Prosecution must support each charge with concrete and tangible
proof establishing substantial grounds to believe that Thorn committed
the crime charged.[^1] The Defence will show that it has not done so for
Count 1. First, Jaku was an emergency treatment, not a biological
experiment; it did not seriously endanger the Titans' health; and it was
therapeutic and in their interest. Secondly, the Titans were not
protected persons for the purposes of Article 8(2)(a). Thirdly, no
international armed conflict existed between 2 and 10 April 2025, and
the treatment had no nexus to any conflict. Finally, Thorn did not aid,
abet or assist any crime with the required purpose and knowledge.

### **Jaku was an emergency treatment, not a biological experiment**

A biological experiment is conduct whose primary purpose is to study the
as yet unknown effects of a product on the human body.[^2] The
prohibition therefore turns on purpose and design, not on the novelty of
the substance. It does not prevent doctors from trying new therapeutic
methods dictated by the wish to improve the patient's condition,[^3] and
the drafters intended precisely to preserve that freedom.[^4] If every
first use of a new drug were an experiment, that exception would have no
content. Purpose is assessed objectively, by asking whether the design
and implementation of the programme are reasonable in relation to its
stated objective,[^5] and a programme may pursue goals beyond its
primary objective without losing its genuine character.[^6] The
post-World War II cases show what a biological experiment looks like:
prisoners in the enemy's power, deliberately infected to test vaccines,
by doctors who took no risk themselves.[^7] Jaku bears none of these
features. It was given to dying soldiers because conventional treatment
had failed.[^8] Every patient received it, including the commanding
officer, who was suffering from the same illness. Thorn was one more
patient, not one more test subject.[^9] The dose was adjusted on the
doctor's advice because many soldiers had not yet recovered, and the
injections stopped as soon as all 40 had regained full health.[^10]
There was no control group, no selection of subjects and no collection
of data for the study. The medical examinations recorded recovery, as
every course of treatment does. Measured by the Whaling test, that
design and implementation were reasonable in relation to one objective:
restoring the soldiers' health. Dr Loff's further hopes for the drug,
such as immunity, strength and national prestige, were secondary goals
that did not change its primary purpose, and the description of Jaku as
'ready for human trials' concerned its stage of development, not the
purpose for which it was given here.[^11] As such, the administration of
Jaku was purely for emergency treatment and does not constitute a
biological experiment.

### **The treatment did not seriously endanger the Titans' health**

Whether an intervention seriously endangers health is judged by its
foreseeable consequences 'having regard to the state of health of the
person'.[^12] The standard is therefore relative: for a patient facing
death, the relevant comparison is with the danger of withholding
treatment. Serious harm to mental health requires more than passing
symptoms. On the Prosecution's own authority, it must result in a grave
and long-term disadvantage to a person's ability to lead a normal and
constructive life.[^13] Inhuman treatment likewise requires severe
physical or mental pain or suffering.[^14] The stricter regime of
Article 11 of Additional Protocol I protects only persons 'in the power
of the adverse Party' or otherwise deprived of liberty.[^15] Article
10(1) imposes a general duty to respect and protect all the wounded and
sick, but it does not extend the experiment regime of Article 11 to a
Party's own forces, which the drafters deliberately confined to persons
in the adversary's power.[^16] For soldiers in comas or 'holding on for
their lives', the danger lay in the illness, not in the cure.[^17] Jaku
woke the four comatose soldiers within three days and restored all 40 to
full health within eight, with performance that 'exceeded
expectations'.[^18] Headaches subsided, and the remaining disorientation
and paranoia were 'sporadic' and, on the medical advice received,
'temporary'.[^19] Far from suffering a grave and long-term disadvantage,
the Titans were 'seemingly unaffected' by the conditions that had
defeated them before, and they went on to scale an escarpment undetected
and destroy a radar installation.[^20] The fatigue was reported three
months after the charged period, and the July paranoia took the form of
concern that the Titans' covert mission had been exposed.[^21] As such,
the second element is not established.

### **The treatment was therapeutic, medically justified and in the Titans' interest**

*Salus aegroti suprema lex*: the welfare of the patient is the supreme
law of medicine. When proven treatments are ineffective, a physician
may, after seeking expert advice, use an unproven intervention that
offers hope of 'saving life, re-establishing health or alleviating
suffering'.[^22] A measure that is a therapeutic necessity cannot, as a
general rule, be regarded as inhuman or degrading, provided that the
necessity is convincingly shown to exist.[^23] A doctor who acts in
accordance with a responsible and logically defensible body of medical
opinion acts properly,[^24] and the preservation of life in an emergency
is the paramount duty of every doctor.[^25] Consent confirms the
therapeutic character. The Elements of Article 8(2)(a)(ii), unlike those
of Article 8(2)(b)(x), do not exclude consent,[^26] and any ambiguity in
the provision must be resolved in the accused's favour.[^27] Legal
systems across the world permit necessary treatment without consent in
emergency circumstances: England on grounds of necessity and best
interests;[^28] Canada, under the emergency doctrine;[^29] India, which
is strict on the rule that consent to one procedure does not extend to
another, yields to the need to save life,[^30] and the United
States.[^31] Even where an intervention is classified as research,
United States and European Union law permit it without consent in an
emergency.[^32] That uniform practice is a general principle of law and
recognised as a source of law under the Rome Statute.[^33] By contrast,
*VC v Slovakia* concerned the sterilisation of a conscious and competent
patient in circumstances where no emergency existed at all.[^34] Jaku
met every part of this test. Regular treatment had failed, the soldiers
were dying, and the head of Damaroi's leading biomedical institute
advised that Jaku could restore their health, which it did.[^35] The
necessity was therefore convincingly shown by the record itself. The
Prosecution's theory of enhancement does not withstand the facts.
Thorn's remark that the Titans 'need to be stronger' was made before
Jaku was ever proposed. The dose was raised 'for optimal results'
because many soldiers still suffered symptoms, and the injections
stopped once recovery was complete.[^36] President Veyar's public
description of the treatment as 'for rehabilitation only' is consistent
with that record.[^37] The conscious soldiers agreed to the treatment,
and the four comatose soldiers could not consent but were a
life-threatening emergency.[^38] For the conscious soldiers, adjusting
the dose within a course of treatment they had already accepted required
no fresh consent, and the same emergency principle continued to apply to
the comatose soldiers.[^39] Thorn did not purport to supply a consent
the soldiers had withheld, but authorised, as their commander, the
continuation of a course of treatment on medical advice. In the further
alternative Thorn's initial approval was necessary and reasonable to
avoid the imminent threat of death to his soldiers, a threat beyond his
control, and he intended no greater harm than the one he sought to
avoid.[^40] As such, the third element is not established.

### **The Titans were not protected persons for the purposes of Article 8(2)(a)**

Article 8(2)(a) criminalizes grave breaches against persons 'protected
under the provisions of the relevant Geneva Convention'.[^41] The
perpetrator 'needs only to know that the victim belonged to an adverse
party to the conflict', and '\[t\]his footnote also applies to the
corresponding element in each crime under article 8(2)(a)'.[^42] The
footnote relaxes the knowledge required as to nationality precisely
because protected status presupposes that the victim belongs to the
adverse party; the drafters of every grave breach therefore assumed an
enemy victim. The treaties must be read in that context and with the
rest of the Geneva system,[^43] which ties protected status to falling
into the power of the other side.[^44]

The ICTY has consistently defined protected status by allegiance to the
opposing party. *Tadić* and *Delalić* extended protection beyond formal
nationality only to victims whose allegiance lay with the other side,
and neither extended it to a party's own forces.[^45] The law of armed
conflict does not, as a rule, protect a party's own forces against their
own State.[^46] National courts agree: the Privy Council, on appeal from
Malaysia, held that nationals of the detaining State could not claim
prisoner-of-war protection against that State.[^47] So does the first
precedent on medical experiments: in the Medical Trial, experiments on
German nationals were punished as crimes against humanity, and only
those on prisoners of war and civilians of occupied territories, meaning
'persons in the power of an adverse party', were war crimes.[^48]
Articles 8(2)(b)(x)-2 and 8(2)(e)(xi)-2 needed an express requirement
that the victim be in the power of an 'adverse' or 'another' party
because, unlike Article 8(2)(a), they contain no protected-person
element. In Article 8(2)(a), the same requirement is supplied by the
protected-person element and footnote 33.[^49] Even the *Ntaganda*
Appeals Chamber, which contrasted the no protected-status element in
Articles 8(2)(b)(xxii) and 8(2)(e)(vi) with 8(2)(a) did not consider
footnote 33 which applies to every crime under Article 8(2)(a) and
identified no case applying that provision to a perpetrator's own
forces.[^50] Even if the Elements are capable of two interpretations,
Article 22(2) requires any ambiguity resolved in favour of the
accused.[^51]

The Titans were Damaroi's own soldiers, treated by Damaroi's own medical
service in a Damaran hospital.[^52] They had not fallen into the power
of the adversary and owed allegiance to no party other than their own.
Thorn knew that they belonged to his own side so the awareness required
by Element 5 is also absent.[^53] As such, Elements 4 and 5 are not
established.

### **No international armed conflict existed between 2 and 10 April 2025, and the treatment had no nexus to any conflict**

An international armed conflict requires 'armed hostilities between
States through their respective armed forces'.[^54] However low the
threshold, some resort to armed force against another State is
required,[^55] and State practice supports a minimum level of
hostilities.[^56] International law has long distinguished genuine uses
of armed force from 'mere frontier incidents'. Although the
International Court of Justice drew that distinction in the law of
self-defence, it reflects the same requirement that force be directed
against another State,[^57] and Armed Activities, on which the
Prosecution relies, concerned a large-scale military intervention and
occupation.[^58] Even where an international armed conflict exists, the
conduct must be associated with it: the conflict must have played a
substantial part in the perpetrator's ability to commit the crime, his
decision to commit it, the manner of its commission or its purpose,[^59]
and a relevant factor is whether the victim belonged to the opposing
party.[^60]

Before 2 April, there had been no hostilities at all. The Titans engaged
no Pallavon forces or civilians and turned back because of disease.[^61]
The fire Thorn set on an uninhabited riverbank was treated by Pallavon
itself as a natural event and never investigated.[^62] The five soldiers
who continued into Pallavon never engaged any Pallavon force, and
forensic evidence shows that they died of the disease in early April.
Stranded soldiers dying of illness do not conduct hostilities.[^63]
Neither State acted as though it were at war. As late as 22 April,
Pallavon's President declared that her country had 'always maintained
good relations with Damaroi', and the first armed exchange occurred at
Santo in June.[^64] Nor did the treatment have any nexus to a conflict.
The treatment of Damaroi's sick soldiers in a Damaran hospital by a
Damaran doctor is exactly what would have occurred in peacetime, and the
victims were not enemy nationals.[^65] Thorn's remark that the Titans
'need to be stronger' was made before Jaku was ever proposed; it
expressed general resolve, not a plan to experiment.[^66] As such,
Elements 6 and 7 are not established.

###  **Thorn did not aid, abet or otherwise assist the crime with the required purpose and knowledge**

Accessory liability presupposes a principal crime.[^67] Article 25(3)(c)
further requires that the accessory act 'for the purpose of
facilitating' the crime, a subjective threshold that goes beyond the
knowledge required by Article 30.[^68] The ad hoc tribunals' decisions
on 'specific direction' applied a customary standard of knowledge, not
the statutory requirement of purpose that the drafters deliberately
chose.[^69] The phrase derives from national law, where the accomplice
must 'in some sort associate himself with the venture' and have advance
knowledge of the circumstances that make the conduct criminal.[^70] Even
moral support must have a substantial effect on the crime and be given
with knowledge that it assists the crime.[^71] The accessory must also
know of every material element, including the non-therapeutic character
of the experiment.[^72] Recklessness and *dolus eventualis* do not
suffice.[^73] Milch was acquitted because the evidence did not show that
he knew of the experiments' criminal character,[^74] and an honest
mistake of fact negates the mental element.[^75]

The principal, Dr Loff, administered Jaku believing that it 'could help
restore their health'.[^76] Thorn's purpose was to save his soldiers,
not to facilitate an experiment. His words show deference to medical
Judgment: 'if the Doc says it's good to go, then it can't be wrong ...
if you think it'll help, I say we give it to the whole group'. The drug
was obtained, prescribed and administered by others.[^77] Thorn relied
on an expert's assurance that the treatment would help and that its side
effects were temporary, and he took the drug himself while ill. No one
who believed it to be a dangerous experiment would have injected
himself.[^78] Any error was one of fact, and it negates the mental
element. Pre-Trial Chambers have declined to confirm charges where the
Prosecution's proof fell short of this standard,[^79] and Count 1 should
not be confirmed.

## **GENERAL THORN IS NOT INDIVIDUALLY CRIMINALLY RESPONSIBLE UNDER ARTICLE 25(3)(b) FOR THE WAR CRIME OF INTENTIONALLY DIRECTING ATTACKS AGAINST CIVILIAN OBJECTS UNDER ARTICLE 8(2)(b)(ii)**

The Defence does not contest the contextual elements of Count 2, but the
Prosecution has not established the remaining elements.[^80] First, the
three schools were military objectives by purpose, and Article 52(3) of
AP I does not displace that purpose. Secondly, Thorn did not intend to
attack civilian objects, and any shortfall in verification concerns
precautions, not the crime charged. Finally, Thorn did not order,
solicit or induce the attack.

### **The contextual elements do not determine Count 2**

Elements 4 and 5 require that the conduct took place in the context of
and was associated with an international armed conflict, and that the
perpetrator was aware of the factual circumstances establishing it.[^81]
For present purposes, the Defence does not contest that the strike of 22
July 2025 satisfied those elements. They do not, however, establish the
crime charged. The Prosecution must still prove that the three schools
were civilian objects, that those who carried out the attack intended
civilian objects to be its object, and that Thorn ordered, solicited or
induced it.[^82]

### **The three schools were military objectives by purpose**

Under Article 52(2) of AP I, an object is a military objective if, by
its nature, location, purpose or use, it makes an effective contribution
to military action and its destruction offers a definite military
advantage in the circumstances ruling at the time. Both requirements are
cumulative, and the definition is customary.[^83] A civilian object
ceases to be civilian once, by its location, purpose or use, it becomes
a military objective. Status changes with the circumstances.[^84] Under
*Ut res magis valeat quam pereat*, 'purpose' must be given its own
meaning, for if it merely repeated 'use' it would be surplusage.[^85]
Purpose concerns the intended future use of an object, while use
concerns its present function, and purpose must rest on concrete
indicators rather than mere suspicion.[^86] In *Hirgigo*, the
Eritrea--Ethiopia Claims Commission agreed that purpose 'means the
future intended use of an object'. It held that a power station not yet
producing power made an effective contribution to military action as the
'intended replacement' for capacity already serving military objectives,
and it treated the adversary's own conduct as evidence of the object's
military significance.[^87] The Defence relies on *Hirgigo* only for the
limited proposition that an object need not yet be functioning where its
intended military purpose is concrete. Even President van Houtte, who
dissented on the facts, accepted that an object which 'made or could
make' an effective contribution may qualify, provided the basis for that
conclusion is sufficiently specified.[^88] The military advantage must
be concrete and perceptible, and it is assessed in the context of the
military operations as a whole.[^89] A defending State must avoid
locating military objectives within or near densely populated
areas.[^90] The Defence does not invoke military necessity as an
independent justification. It relies on the definition in Article 52(2)
itself.[^91] The schools' military purpose was concrete, specific and
objectively established. First, Pallavon's Cabinet formally decided to
repurpose schools larger than two hectares and within one kilometre of
the railway 'as new sites to train recruits and prepare operations', in
order to use their grounds, classrooms and access to the railway 'to
receive military equipment'.[^92] Those criteria identified exactly 20
schools, including the three attacked. The designation was therefore
specific to the objects and not a general prediction. Secondly, the
decision was implemented. Twelve of the 20 schools were initially
converted into military facilities, and after attacks on them, five
further qualifying schools were suspended, repurposed, and struck after
the Titans confirmed their conversion, with each destroyed facility
replaced by the next qualifying school.[^93] Thirdly, the final three
were dismissed from teaching and their grounds evacuated, the same first
step that had preceded every earlier conversion.[^94] Fourthly, Pallavon
was mobilising over two million recruits, receiving weapons and trainers
by rail, and converting civilian buildings, including town halls, into
training camps.[^95] The conversion of town halls does not suggest
alternatives to the schools. It confirms Pallavon's systematic
conversion of civilian buildings to military use. Like the power station
in Hirgigo, the schools were the intended replacement for capacity
already lost, and Pallavon's own Cabinet had attributed military
significance to them. These facts supply precisely the specification
that President van Houtte found lacking. Nor can the schools be compared
with hospitals or stadiums near a railway, because no Cabinet had
decided to convert those objects. Their destruction also offered a
definite military advantage: it denied Pallavon the capacity to
reconstitute its training infrastructure in the middle of a mass
mobilisation, a capacity it had exercised five times in a single
week.[^96] As such, the three schools were military objectives by
purpose.

### **Article 52(3) does not displace the evidence of military purpose**

The presumption in Article 52(3) of AP I applies where there is doubt
whether a school *'is being used'* to make an effective contribution to
military action.[^97] The provision names present use and says nothing
of purpose. Reading it to cancel the separate purpose limb of Article
52(2) would deprive that limb of effect.[^98] The Rome Statute itself
protects buildings dedicated to education only 'provided they are not
military objectives'.[^99] The Safe Schools Guidelines do not alter
this. They are expressly non-binding and without prejudice to existing
international law, and endorsement of the Safe Schools Declaration is a
political commitment. Such instruments may inform standards that the law
itself incorporates, such as the generally accepted medical standards
referred to in Article 11 of AP I.[^100] But neither the Guidelines nor
the Declaration can amend the definition of a military objective agreed
by States in Article 52(2) and recognised as customary.[^101] The
Defence accepts that schools are ordinarily civilian and that the
presumption governs doubt about present use. The agreed facts record
that, on 20 July, 'no positive action had yet been taken to use the
premises for military purposes'.[^102] That finding concerns use, not
purpose. Purpose is by definition directed to the future, so it cannot
depend on conversion having already begun. Otherwise the purpose limb of
Article 52(2) would add nothing to use. Purpose is still assessed
objectively, by what Pallavon did as well as what it decided. Its
decision had been implemented at 17 of the 20 qualifying sites, and at
the final three the same first step, evacuation, had already been
taken.[^103] President Veyar's doubt likewise concerned only whether the
schools were 'actually being used', and Thorn's answer addressed their
purpose: 'Even if they are not being used now, their purpose is
clear'.[^104] On purpose, the Cabinet decision, the conversion cycle and
the evacuations left no genuine doubt. Nothing in the record shows that
either State endorsed the Safe Schools Declaration, and Thorn's
reasoning was not that necessity overrides the law, but that the schools
met the definition of a military objective. That is the opposite of the
doctrine of *Kriegsraison*. As such, Article 52(3) does not displace the
schools' military purpose.

### **Thorn did not intend to attack civilian objects**

Element 3 requires that the perpetrator '*intended* such civilian
objects to be the object of the attack'.[^105] Because Count 2 is
charged as ordering, that element must first be met by those who carried
out the attack. The record does not identify who launched the missiles,
let alone what they knew about the targets, so their intent cannot be
established.[^106] Thorn's own mental state matters only to whether he
ordered a crime, and he too lacked the required intent. The question is
not whether Thorn meant the missiles to strike the schools, but whether
he meant to attack them as civilian objects. During the negotiation of
the Elements, States rejected recklessness for this element. The
perpetrator must mean the objects to be civilian targets or know that
they are civilian.[^107] That standard means virtual certainty, not a
possibility or a risk.[^108] Recklessness and *dolus eventualis* are
excluded.[^109] War crimes require knowledge of the facts that make the
conduct criminal, and military personnel who, acting in good faith on
erroneous information, attack an object they believe to be a military
objective commit no crime.[^110]

Targeting decisions are judged on the information reasonably available
at the time, not with hindsight.[^111] That standard, first applied in
*Rendulic*,[^112] has passed into State practice,[^113] and findings of
unlawful attack cannot rest on standards constructed after the
event.[^114] In *Hanan v Germany*, concerning the Kunduz airstrike, the
European Court of Human Rights accepted that the German Federal
Prosecutor's finding, that a commander who acted on intelligence he
believed reliable lacked criminal intent, rested on an adequate
investigation.[^115] A mistake of fact negates the mental element, and
even a mistake about a normative element of the crime may do so.[^116]
Thorn acted on the Cabinet programme, the 12 initial conversions, the
five confirmed replacements, the replacement pattern and the evacuations
when he concluded that 'every school of that size is being
converted'.[^117] His departure from the earlier practice of
confirmation does not show intent. Confirmation had concerned schools
whose conversion was already underway. For the final three, the question
was their purpose, and waiting for conversion would have allowed
Pallavon to restore the training capacity it had just lost. He proposed
a night strike 'to minimise risk of harming civilians', the very
precaution that IHL requires in attacks on military objectives.[^118] A
commander who believes a site is about to become a military training
facility is not virtually certain that he is attacking a civilian
object. In the alternative, even if the schools were civilian objects,
Thorn was at most mistaken about whether Pallavon would convert these
particular schools imminently. That belief rested on observed facts,
namely the Cabinet decision, the conversions and the evacuations, not on
a guess. Whether the mistake is characterised as factual or mixed, it
negates the mental element. In the further alternative, it is a mistake
about the normative element 'military objective' that equally negates
intent. The deaths of cleaning staff at the third school are
regrettable, but that harm arose from an attack on what Thorn believed
to be a military objective. It could at most raise a question of
excessive incidental harm under Article 8(2)(b)(iv), a crime that has
not been charged.[^119] As such, Element 3 is not established.

### **Any shortfall in verification is a question of precautions, not the crime charged**

Article 8(2)(b)(ii) punishes intentionally directing an attack against
civilian objects. It does not criminalise a failure to verify a target.
The duty to verify arises under AP I,[^120] but the Rome Statute
contains no corresponding war crime, and even AP I treats as grave
breaches only the wilful attacks it lists, not omissions in
precaution.[^121] A duty of conduct cannot be turned into a crime of
intentional attack by relabelling it.[^122] At most, a claim that Thorn
should have waited longer alleges negligence, and negligence is not
intent.[^123] In any event, the duty is to do everything feasible to
verify, meaning what is practicable or practically possible in the
circumstances ruling at the time, taking account of humanitarian and
military considerations.[^124] The law does not demand certainty, but a
reasonable judgment on the information available.[^125]

Thorn met that standard. The Titans had monitored the converted schools
across the country, supplying over 300 images and videos, delivery logs
of weapons and uniforms, and records of night-time activity.[^126] That
intelligence concerned the schools already converted, and it established
the pattern that Thorn then applied to the final three. The Titans had
observed that every conversion began with the school's suspension and
evacuation, and that this step had now occurred at all three
schools.[^127] The President raised his doubt, and Thorn answered it on
that evidence. A commander who weighs a doubt and resolves it on the
information before him is verifying, not ignoring, the question.[^128]
Thorn proposed striking evacuated premises at night, the course least
likely to harm anyone.[^129] As such, any shortfall in verification is a
precautionary question, not the crime charged.

### **Thorn did not order, solicit or induce the attack**

Ordering requires a person in a position of authority who uses that
authority to instruct another to commit the crime.[^130] A formal
relationship is not required, but authority over the person who carries
out the crime must at least be reasonably implied from the
evidence,[^131] and the order must have a direct effect on the
commission of the crime.[^132] Ordering is not advice. It means using
another person under one's authority to carry out the crime.[^133] The
label a narrative gives to a communication is not a finding that these
elements are met,[^134] and Pre-Trial Chambers have declined to confirm
modes of liability unsupported by the evidence.[^135] Soliciting and
inducing likewise require influence that has a direct effect on the
commission of the crime, or that is a substantially contributing factor
to it.[^136] The agreed facts' use of the word 'order' does not settle
the question, because the record identifies no recipient. It does not
show who launched the missiles, who authorised the launch, what chain of
command linked Thorn to the missile force in Como City, or that anyone
there was obliged to act on his word.[^137] Thorn was Chief of the
Damaran Defence Force, but a title does not prove authority over a
particular act. Thorn commanded the Titans in the field, but nothing
shows that he commanded the missile force in Como City. Authority to
order must be reasonably implied from the evidence,[^138] and the
evidence here points elsewhere. At the relevant time, Thorn was deployed
inside Pallavon as the President's 'eyes and ears', tasked to supply
'sound intel and a plan'. The President's words 'I'll leave it with you'
entrusted Thorn with the intelligence and the plan, not with command of
the missile campaign the President himself directed.[^139] Thorn's
earlier contribution took the form of a 'recommendation',[^140] and the
President's question, 'Are you sure we should strike them?', confirms
that Thorn's word was not self-executing and that the decision to launch
remained with the President.[^141] Supplying coordinates is, at most,
assistance, a mode not charged. It is not an order. Nor did Thorn
solicit or induce the attack. The President was already conducting a
missile campaign to sting Pallavon 'where it hurts' and had his own
reasons to continue it.[^142] Thorn spoke as an adviser. He described
the strike as '\[o\]ur best course of action', which is a
recommendation, not a demand.[^143] The President's question, 'Are you
sure we should strike them?', was not a refusal that Thorn overcame, and
nothing records that the President's decision turned on Thorn's reply.
The record therefore does not show that Thorn's assessment, rather than
the President's independent decision, caused the launch, or that Thorn
influenced any person who carried it out. As such, the Prosecution has
not provided concrete and tangible proof of the mode of liability
charged, and Count 2 should not be confirmed.
