
### A. GENERAL THORN IS INDIVIDUALLY CRIMINALLY RESPONSIBLE UNDER ARTICLE 25(3)(c) FOR AIDING, ABETTING OR OTHERWISE ASSISTING IN THE COMMISSION OF THE WAR CRIME OF TORTURE OR INHUMAN TREATMENT, INCLUDING BIOLOGICAL EXPERIMENTS, UNDER ARTICLE 8(2)(a)(ii)

The Prosecution will first establish each element of the war crime under Article 8(2)(a)(ii), and then Thorn's liability under Article 25(3)(c).¹ It will show that Jaku was a biological experiment to which the Titans were subjected; that the experiment seriously endangered their health; and that it was non-therapeutic, medically unjustified and not carried out in their interest. It will then show that the Titans were protected persons and that the conduct was associated with an international armed conflict. Finally, it will show that Thorn aided, abetted or otherwise assisted the crime with the required intent and knowledge.


#### 1. Jaku was a biological experiment to which the Titans were subjected

A biological experiment is conduct whose primary purpose is to study the as yet unknown effects of a product or situation on the human body.² The concept overlaps with the ‘medical or scientific experiments’ prohibited by the Third and Fourth Geneva Conventions, and all four Conventions list biological experiments as a grave breach.³ The prohibition is customary and rests on ‘elementary considerations of humanity’,⁴ and human rights law confirms its core: no one may be subjected ‘without his free consent to medical or scientific experimentation’.⁵ The prohibition therefore turns on the character of the intervention, not on the label its author attaches to it.

Post-war practice confirms that a medical or military purpose does not change that character. In the Medical Trial, the typhus experiments at Buchenwald were conducted to test vaccines for the German armed forces; the Tribunal nevertheless convicted Rose, who actively furthered them, and Handloser, the Chief of the Medical Services, who failed to stop them.⁶ The Khabarovsk tribunal reached the same conclusion about the Imperial Japanese Army's programme.⁷ An experiment requires no laboratory design, control group or written protocol: it is any systematic intervention seeking generalisable information about a human being, whether it is labelled ‘biological’ or ‘medical’.⁸ Whether an intervention genuinely serves its stated purpose is assessed objectively, by asking whether its ‘design and implementation are reasonable in relation to achieving its stated objectives’.⁹ The prohibition exists to prevent protected persons being used as guinea-pigs,¹⁰ and ‘subjected’ in Element 1 must be read with Articles 12(2) and 50 of GC I, which protect the wounded and sick from precisely this.¹¹

Jaku meets this definition. It was an ‘experimental drug’ in the ‘early stages of development’, declared ‘ready for human trials’. Its stated aims included enhancing ‘immunity’ and ‘muscle strength and endurance’ and putting Damaroi ‘back on the map’ in biomedical science, and its results were measured in medical exams that ‘exceeded expectations’ and ‘surpass[ed] all initial predictions’.¹² Administering such a drug was necessarily a test of its unknown effects, exactly as the typhus vaccines were. All 40 returning soldiers, four of them in comas, received the drug; by taking it himself, Thorn became one more subject of the test, not its physician.¹³ As such, the Titans were subjected to a particular biological experiment.


#### 2. The experiment seriously endangered the Titans' physical and mental health

Element 2 requires that the experiment seriously endanger physical or mental health or integrity; a concrete danger of serious harm suffices, and the harm need not materialise.¹⁴ The drafters adopted the threshold of Article 11(4) of Additional Protocol I. The danger is judged objectively, by the foreseeable consequences in light of the victims' state of health, so a body already weakened by disease is more vulnerable to an untested drug, not less.¹⁵ The perpetrator need not personally complete the value judgement that the danger was ‘serious’.¹⁶ Serious psychiatric disturbance qualifies as serious harm to mental health, and it need not be permanent, provided it goes beyond temporary unhappiness.¹⁷ No custody requirement limits this element. Articles 12 and 50 of GC I contain none, and Additional Protocol I itself requires that all the wounded and sick be respected and protected ‘to whichever Party they belong’; the ‘power of the adverse Party’ condition of Article 11 cannot be imported into it.¹⁸

The danger here was not only foreseeable but known. After the half doses, many soldiers suffered ‘severe headaches, disorientation and episodes of paranoia’. The dose was nevertheless doubled on a bare assurance that the symptoms were ‘temporary’, without notice to or consent from the soldiers. The disorientation and paranoia persisted after the injections ended, and by July all Titans reported paranoia, several suffered increasing fatigue, and Thorn himself felt the drug's benefits ‘wearing off’.¹⁹ These were not the passing effects of a cure: they outlasted the injections by three months. A sick patient's health is seriously endangered when an untested drug is doubled despite known neurological and psychiatric side-effects. As such, the second element is satisfied.


#### 3. The Jaku experiment was non-therapeutic, medically unjustified and not carried out in the Titans' interest

Element 3 requires that the experiment's intent was non-therapeutic and that it was neither justified by medical reasons nor carried out in the victims' interest. Article 12 of GC I prohibits biological experiments on the wounded and sick outright.²⁰ Article 13 of GC III, Article 32 of GC IV and Article 11(1) of AP I express the same limit: no procedure may be performed unless it is indicated by the person's state of health and consistent with generally accepted medical standards.²¹ The exception for new therapeutic methods is narrow. It permits them only where they are justified on medical grounds and dictated solely by the wish to improve the patient's condition.²² Generally accepted standards are those reflected in the international ethical guidelines on research involving human beings.²³ Element 3 describes the character of the experiment, not the perpetrator's state of mind; it is a material element in the form of a circumstance.²⁴ Footnote 46 of the Elements, read with Rule 92 of the ICRC Customary Study, confirms the content of the applicable medical standards,²⁵ and the perpetrator need only know the underlying facts, not conclude for himself that the experiment was ‘non-therapeutic’.²⁶

Medical justification is strictly confined. New remedies may be used only once they have been ‘satisfactorily proved to be innocuous’, and only for purely therapeutic purposes.²⁷ Where a measure is said to be a therapeutic necessity, that necessity must be ‘convincingly shown to exist’.²⁸ Calling a procedure ‘treatment’ does not settle its character. A surgeon who operates without anaesthetic treats his patient, but treats him inhumanely, and biological experiments on the wounded and sick violate the duty of humane treatment even where they cause no death or serious injury.²⁹ Nor can a doctor's duty to preserve life excuse an experiment: that duty obliges the State and its doctors to provide care, and humane treatment is an obligation of result admitting no exception.³⁰

Consent cannot cure an experiment of this kind. The Elements of Article 8(2)(a)(ii), unlike those of Article 8(2)(b)(x), do not expressly exclude consent. But the wounded and sick cannot validly consent to an experiment that endangers their health, because consent can justify only treatment of an eminently therapeutic nature.³¹ The Medical Trial made voluntary consent the first condition of any lawful experiment. It held that the duty to secure consent rests personally on each person who initiates, directs or engages in the experiment, and that it cannot be delegated.³² Consent must be informed, knowing and voluntary,³³ and Additional Protocol I prohibits such experiments even with consent.³⁴ Even an unproven intervention in clinical practice requires the informed consent of the patient or a legally authorised representative, and treatment without informed consent may itself be inhuman.³⁵ Leading national decisions confirm that patients must be told of material risks, that consent to one procedure does not extend to another, and that an intervention presented as treatment cannot conceal a research purpose.³⁶ Even where national law allows consent to be waived for service members, only the President may do so, and only in writing.³⁷

Measured against these standards, Jaku's purpose was enhancement, not cure. The aim from the outset was to make the Titans ‘stronger’ and ‘even mightier than last time’, and Jaku was chosen because it would increase ‘muscle strength and endurance’. The dose was doubled ‘for optimal results’ only after Thorn had felt ‘newfound strength’, and while some soldiers had already recovered. The injections stopped once the group was ‘deemed fit’ and its combat readiness ‘exceptional’, despite persisting side-effects, and the effect was later felt to be ‘wearing off’, as an enhancement does and a cure does not.³⁸ The measure of success was combat fitness, not health. President Veyar's later claim that the treatment was ‘for rehabilitation only’ carries no weight: it was made publicly after a leak, whereas the decision itself was taken as a ‘top-secret matter of national security’.³⁹ Jaku had been proved neither innocuous nor necessary, and nothing explains why the dose had to be doubled after some soldiers had already recovered, other than Thorn's own ‘newfound strength’.⁴⁰

Nor was there valid consent. The four comatose soldiers could not consent. No soldier consented to the doubled dose, and nothing in the record shows that the conscious soldiers were told Jaku was experimental. *Volenti non fit injuria* cannot apply where the victim did not, or could not, agree. *Nemo dat quod non habet*: Thorn's approval ‘on behalf of the Titans’ could not supply a consent that belonged only to the soldiers themselves, and no written waiver was ever issued.⁴¹ As such, the third element is satisfied.


#### 4. The Titans were protected persons, and Thorn was aware of the factual circumstances establishing that status

The victims must be protected under one or more of the 1949 Geneva Conventions; the Elements impose no requirement of custody or control.⁴² GC I protects members of the armed forces of a Party to the conflict who are wounded or sick,⁴³ meaning persons who need medical care because of disease or other disorder and who refrain from any act of hostility.⁴⁴ Whether a person is ‘wounded or sick’ is a matter of common sense and good faith, and soldiers who remain symptomatic in hospital are plainly ‘sick’.⁴⁵

That protection extends to a Party's own soldiers. Article 12 of GC I protects the wounded and sick ‘in all circumstances’ and without adverse distinction, and being ‘in the power of a Party’ includes being in the power of one's own Party.⁴⁶ Article 5 concerns persons who have ‘fallen into the hands of the enemy’, but Article 13 contains no such condition, which confirms that not every protected person must be in enemy hands.⁴⁷ The Appeals Chamber has accordingly recognised that the protection of GC I and GC II is not confined to members of enemy forces, but covers the wounded and sick of a Party's own armed forces.⁴⁸

The Elements point the same way. Footnote 33 does not define who is protected. It relaxes the mental element ‘with respect to nationality’, so that the perpetrator need only know that the victim belonged to an adverse party where protected status turns on nationality, as it does under GC IV. Protected status under GC I turns on the victim's condition, not his nationality, and the ICTY has consistently read protected status by substance rather than form, so as to give the Conventions their purpose of maximum protection.⁴⁹ *Expressio unius est exclusio alterius*: the drafters expressly required that the victim be in the power of an ‘adverse’ or ‘another’ party in Articles 8(2)(b)(x)-2 and 8(2)(e)(xi)-2, but deliberately omitted that requirement from Article 8(2)(a)(ii).⁵⁰ As to awareness, the perpetrator need only know the factual circumstances establishing protected status; he need not make the legal evaluation himself.⁵¹

The Titans fall squarely within this protection. All 40 returning soldiers had contracted an unknown disease with severe symptoms, and four were in induced comas when the injections began; none was taking part in hostilities.⁵² Thorn knew all of this. He hand-picked and led the platoon, suffered the same illness, and told the President that ‘[s]ome of the guys are holding on for their lives’.⁵³ As such, Elements 4 and 5 are satisfied.


#### 5. The conduct took place in the context of and was associated with an international armed conflict, of which Thorn was aware

Common Article 2 applies to any armed conflict between two or more High Contracting Parties, even if one of them does not recognise a state of war; no threshold of intensity is required.⁵⁴ An international armed conflict (‘IAC’) exists whenever there is a ‘resort to armed force between States’,⁵⁵ whatever the reasons for or the intensity of the confrontation, and without any declaration or recognition; its existence depends on the facts on the ground.⁵⁶ It includes ‘armed hostilities between States through their respective armed forces’.⁵⁷ Any difference between two States leading to the intervention of armed forces is an armed conflict, whatever its duration or the number of victims,⁵⁸ and it is irrelevant whether the State attacked resists, or is even aware of the intervention.⁵⁹ The International Court of Justice's distinction between an ‘armed attack’ and a ‘mere frontier incident’ concerns the threshold for self-defence, not the existence of an armed conflict.⁶⁰ Once an IAC exists, IHL applies throughout the territory of the parties, not only where fighting takes place.⁶¹

The conduct must also be associated with the conflict. The nexus is assessed objectively and does not require geographic or temporal proximity to the fighting.⁶² It is met where the conflict played a substantial part in the perpetrator's ability or decision to commit the crime, the manner of its commission, or its purpose.⁶³ Relevant factors include whether the perpetrator was a combatant, whether the act served the ultimate goal of a military campaign, and whether it was committed as part of official duties.⁶⁴ As to awareness, the perpetrator need only know the facts indicating hostilities; no legal evaluation of the existence or character of the conflict is required.⁶⁵

An IAC existed by mid-March 2025 at the latest. Damaran soldiers, led by the Chief of the Damaran Defence Force, crossed the River Targa into Pallavon on a mission to dismantle its air defences so that Damaroi could ‘take control of the country’. As he withdrew, Thorn ordered five soldiers to continue the mission inside Pallavon, and he set fire to the Pallavon riverbank ‘for “easier access next time”’. Those soldiers remained in Pallavon into early April, within the charged period.⁶⁶ This armed incursion by State forces into another State's territory, carried out to prepare its conquest, was a resort to armed force between States. The conflict then intensified into open hostilities, including the seizure of Santo, the destruction of a Pallavon radar installation, and missile strikes from Damaran territory.⁶⁷ The injections, although administered in Damaroi, fall within that conflict.

The injections were also associated with the conflict. Thorn acted as Chief of the Damaran Defence Force. The injections were intended to make the Titans ‘stronger’ for when ‘we go back in’, and the squadron at once resumed training for Operation Titan 2.0. With no word from the five soldiers left in Pallavon, the operation was, so far as Thorn knew, still under way, and he had planned and led the incursion himself.⁶⁸ As such, Elements 6 and 7 are satisfied.


#### 6. Thorn aided, abetted or otherwise assisted in the commission of the crime with the required mental element

Article 25(3)(c) makes a person liable as an accessory where he aids, abets or otherwise assists in the commission, or attempted commission, of a crime by another.⁶⁹ It covers any form of assistance, physical or moral, including approval and encouragement, provided for the purpose of facilitating the crime.⁷⁰ The assistance need not have a substantial effect on the commission of the crime; it must be given with the purpose of facilitating it and with awareness of it.⁷¹ ‘Purpose’ refers to the purpose of facilitating the conduct, not to a desire that the crime occur: an accomplice need not wish for the outcome. International courts have also rejected any further requirement that the assistance be ‘specifically directed’ to the crime.⁷² The accessory's liability depends on the commission of the material elements by the principal, and the non-therapeutic character of an experiment is an objective circumstance, not a matter of the principal's personal belief.⁷³

The material elements must be committed with intent and knowledge;⁷⁴ recklessness and *dolus eventualis* are excluded.⁷⁵ The post-war tribunals likewise required intent as to the material elements.⁷⁶ In *Milch*, conviction required proof that the accused knew of the experiments, knew that they were criminal in scope and execution, and had that knowledge in time to act. Milch was acquitted because he did not participate in the experiments, did not direct them, and did not know of them.⁷⁷ National law asks the same question: whether the accomplice had advance knowledge of the facts at a time when he could still refuse to take part.⁷⁸

Applying these principles, Dr Loff was the principal. She administered Jaku in a State-directed programme that the President commissioned as a ‘top-secret matter of national security’, knowing that the drug was experimental and hoping that it would enhance strength and restore Damaroi's scientific standing.⁷⁹ Thorn did not order the injections; he approved them, which is conduct squarely within Article 25(3)(c). His assistance was decisive at both stages, so it would meet even a test of substantial effect. He agreed to Jaku ‘without hesitation’ and proposed that it be given to ‘the whole group’, and he later approved the doubled dose ‘on behalf of the Titans’, after which Dr Loff moved to full injections.⁸⁰ Without his approvals, neither phase of the experiment would have proceeded as it did.

Thorn is also the opposite of Milch. He knew that Jaku was experimental, because the President passed on everything Dr Loff had said, including that it was in the ‘early stages of development’ and ‘ready for human trials’. He knew of the side-effects before he approved the higher dose. And he knew that no soldier had been asked to consent to it, because he gave the approval himself. Each approval was given with advance knowledge of these facts, at a time when he could still have refused.⁸¹ *Actus non facit reum nisi mens sit rea*: here both the act and the guilty mind are present. Thus, there are substantial grounds to believe that Thorn is individually criminally responsible under Article 25(3)(c) for the war crime under Article 8(2)(a)(ii).


### B. GENERAL THORN IS INDIVIDUALLY CRIMINALLY RESPONSIBLE UNDER ARTICLE 25(3)(b) FOR ORDERING, SOLICITING OR INDUCING THE WAR CRIME OF INTENTIONALLY DIRECTING ATTACKS AGAINST CIVILIAN OBJECTS UNDER ARTICLE 8(2)(b)(ii)

The Prosecution will first establish that the strike of 22 July 2025 occurred in the context of and was associated with an international armed conflict. It will then show that the three schools were civilian objects, and that Thorn directed an attack against them, intending them to be its object. It will next show that Thorn ordered the attack or, alternatively, induced it. Finally, it will show that no ground excludes his criminal responsibility.⁸²


#### 1. The contextual elements of Article 8(2)(b)(ii) are satisfied

Elements 4 and 5 require that the conduct took place in the context of and was associated with an international armed conflict, and that the perpetrator was aware of the factual circumstances establishing it.⁸³ An IAC exists whenever there is a resort to armed force between States, whatever its intensity.⁸⁴ The conduct is associated with the conflict where the conflict played a substantial part in the perpetrator's decision, purpose, manner or ability to commit it.⁸⁵ Awareness requires knowledge of the facts, not a legal classification of the conflict.⁸⁶

The Prosecution has shown that an IAC existed by mid-March 2025.⁸⁷ In any event, its existence by 22 July 2025 is beyond dispute. Damaran forces had seized Santo, killing 45 of its defenders; Pallavon had ordered mass conscription; the Titans had destroyed a Pallavon radar installation; and Damaroi had repeatedly launched missiles from its own territory against Pallavon's training facilities.⁸⁸ The strike was closely associated with that conflict. As the President's ‘eyes and ears’, Thorn gathered military intelligence inside Pallavon, selected alleged enemy training sites, supplied their coordinates to Damaran missile forces, and justified the strike as preventing the schools' use ‘against us’.⁸⁹ Thorn led Damaran forces in Pallavon and directed these operations himself, so he knew the facts establishing the conflict. As such, Elements 4 and 5 are satisfied.


#### 2. The three schools were civilian objects, not military objectives

Under Article 52(2) of AP I, an object is a military objective only where, by its nature, location, purpose or use, it makes an effective contribution to military action and its total or partial destruction offers a definite military advantage in the circumstances ruling at the time. Both requirements are cumulative, and all other objects are civilian objects.⁹⁰ The contribution must exist in the actual situation, not in the abstract: suitability for some later military use is not enough.⁹¹ ‘Purpose’ refers to an object's intended future use, but that purpose must be concrete, specific to the object attacked and objectively established at the time of the attack; a general pattern or a prediction of future use cannot suffice.⁹² The military advantage must likewise be concrete and perceptible, not potential or speculative.⁹³

Schools enjoy particular protection. Article 52(3) of AP I provides that, in case of doubt whether an object normally dedicated to civilian purposes, expressly including a school, is being used to make an effective contribution to military action, it shall be presumed not to be so used.⁹⁴ Any separate claim of future military purpose must then independently satisfy Article 52(2). Buildings dedicated to education are separately protected by the Rome Statute,⁹⁵ and the Guidelines endorsed by more than 100 States through the Safe Schools Declaration provide that schools must never be destroyed in order to deprive the adversary of the ability to use them in the future.⁹⁶ The removal of civilians from the vicinity of potential targets is a precaution IHL requires of the defender, not evidence of military use.⁹⁷ Finally, military necessity is reflected in, and limited by, the definition of a military objective. Once an object is civilian, no independent plea of necessity authorises its attack;⁹⁸ the doctrine of *Kriegsraison* was rejected at Nuremberg,⁹⁹ and distinction between civilian objects and military objectives is a ‘cardinal’ principle of IHL.¹⁰⁰

The final three schools fail both requirements of Article 52(2). As to use, the record distinguishes them from the 17 schools previously struck. Twelve had already been converted into military facilities, and five further schools were struck only after the Titans had observed and confirmed their conversion. By contrast, ‘no positive action had yet been taken’ to use the final three for military purposes.¹⁰¹ The President himself acknowledged that there was no ‘confirmation the schools are actually being used as facilities’, and Thorn conceded: ‘Even if they are not being used now’.¹⁰² *Habemus optimum testem, confitentem reum*. The presumption in Article 52(3) therefore applies with full force, and the dismissal of pupils from the schools, a civilian precaution, cannot rebut it.

As to location, the schools' size, grounds, classrooms and proximity to the railway showed at most their suitability for a possible future conversion. If suitability sufficed, every large hospital or stadium near a railway would be a lawful target.¹⁰³ As to purpose, the Cabinet's decision identified a category of schools, not these three. Only 12 of the 20 qualifying schools were initially converted, and the five later conversions occurred only after attacks on existing facilities. That history shows a possibility of future conversion; it does not show that the final three had already been assigned an actual military purpose on 20 July, particularly as Pallavon had also converted town halls into training camps.¹⁰⁴ *Hirgigo* does not assist the Defence. It concerned a power station under construction for that very function, and even there the Commission required an effective contribution as a ‘first step’,¹⁰⁵ whereas no step towards conversion had been taken at these schools.

Nor did their destruction offer a definite military advantage. Any advantage depended on future contingencies: Pallavon would first have to decide to convert, equip, staff and use the sites. Thorn confirmed that contingency when he proposed their destruction ‘before Pallavon has the chance [to] use them against us’.¹⁰⁶ That is preventive destruction of civilian property in anticipation of its possible future use, which is precisely the *Kriegsraison* reasoning that IHL rejects. As such, the three schools were civilian objects.


#### 3. Thorn directed an attack against the schools and intended them to be its object

An ‘attack’ is an act of violence against the adversary.¹⁰⁷ Article 8(2)(b)(ii) requires that an attack be directed at civilian objects; it does not require that every intended strike succeed.¹⁰⁸ Element 3 requires that the perpetrator intended the civilian objects to be the object of the attack. Under Article 30, he must mean to engage in the conduct and know the factual circumstances that make the objects civilian; he need not make the legal evaluation himself.¹⁰⁹ Those who plan or decide upon an attack must do everything feasible to verify that the objective is neither civilian nor a civilian object, and must cancel or suspend it where it becomes apparent that the objective is not military.¹¹⁰ In case of doubt, only thoroughly verified information will do.¹¹¹ A failure to verify does not by itself prove intent, but it may corroborate an inference of intent drawn from other facts.

The missile barrage was an attack within Article 49(1) of AP I, and the schools were its deliberate objects: it was launched to the coordinates Thorn supplied, and missiles struck all three schools. They were the intended targets, not incidental damage falling under Article 8(2)(b)(iv).¹¹² Thorn intentionally selected those schools while knowing that no target-specific military use or conversion activity had been established. The President expressly warned him that there was no confirmation of use; Thorn accepted that the schools were not being used ‘now’, and ‘gave the order to target all remaining schools’ so that they would be destroyed before Pallavon could use them.¹¹³ *Res ipsa loquitur*: he knew the decisive facts and chose destruction on the basis of anticipated use. His proposal of a night strike confirms that the schools themselves, rather than any incidental damage, were deliberately selected; although it may be presented as a precaution, it cannot transform civilian schools into military objectives.¹¹⁴ His departure from the Titans' own practice corroborates that inference. The five earlier schools had been struck only after their conversion was confirmed, but Thorn proceeded against the final three after being told that confirmation was absent.¹¹⁵ As such, Elements 1 to 3 are satisfied.


#### 4. Thorn ordered the attack or, alternatively, induced it

Under Article 25(3)(b), ordering requires that the accused, being in a position of authority, use that authority to instruct another to commit a crime, that the instruction have a direct effect on its commission, and that the accused be at least aware that the crime will occur in the ordinary course of events.¹¹⁶ A formal superior–subordinate relationship is not indispensable, but actual authority to direct the crime must be established, and it may be inferred from circumstantial evidence.¹¹⁷ *Qui facit per alium facit per se*: a person who orders a crime commits it through another.¹¹⁸ Article 25(3)(b) covers a crime that in fact occurs or is attempted.¹¹⁹ Alternatively, soliciting and inducing cover urging, advising or otherwise influencing another to commit a crime. They require no superior–subordinate relationship, provided the influence has a direct effect on the commission of the crime.¹²⁰

Thorn ordered the attack. His effective authority is shown by his position as Chief of the Damaran Defence Force, by the President's entrustment of intelligence and planning to him (‘get me some sound intel and a plan. I'll leave it with you to figure out what that looks like’), by his selection of the targets, and by his provision of the coordinates.¹²¹ He ‘gave the order to target all remaining schools fitting the criteria, and provided strike coordinates’, and the missiles were launched ‘to coordinates provided by Thorn’.¹²² His instruction was not abstract advice: it identified the targets and supplied the data the strike required. The crime occurred, because missiles struck all three schools; in any event, Article 25(3)(b) also encompasses its attempted commission.

Alternatively, Thorn induced the strike. When the President questioned the attack because military use remained unconfirmed, Thorn pressed the proposal, insisting that ‘[t]he pattern is obvious’, supplied its preventive rationale, proposed its night-time execution, and provided the coordinates. The barrage followed two days later at the coordinates he supplied.¹²³ That sequence permits the inference that his influence had a direct effect on the commission of the crime. As such, there are substantial grounds to believe that Thorn ordered the attack or, alternatively, induced it.


#### 5. No ground excludes Thorn's criminal responsibility

A mistake of fact excludes responsibility only if it negates the mental element.¹²⁴ The classic case is a commander who, acting in good faith on erroneous information, believes a civilian object to be a military objective; the *Rendulic* standard judges him on the information available to him at the time.¹²⁵ A mistake of law is not a ground for excluding responsibility unless it negates the mental element.¹²⁶ Article 31(1)(a) and (b) require that a mental disease, defect or intoxication destroy the person's capacity to appreciate the unlawfulness or nature of his conduct, or to control it.¹²⁷

No evidence shows that Thorn relied on false intelligence that the final schools were already converted or in military use. He was told that military use was unconfirmed and knew that no positive action towards such use had begun. His asserted belief therefore rested on a prediction of future conversion, not on misinformation about present facts.¹²⁸ The Defence may characterise his assessment of the schools' ‘purpose’ as factual, legal or mixed. Whatever its label, it did not negate his awareness of the decisive present facts: no confirmation, no current use and no conversion activity. A speculative prediction of future use cannot erase knowledge of those circumstances.

Nor did Jaku destroy Thorn's capacity. The agreed facts show generalised paranoia and fatigue among the Titans, but no individual evidence that Thorn's capacity was destroyed on 20 July; he had reported weeks earlier that Jaku's benefits were ‘wearing off’.¹²⁹ His structured target assessment, his response to the President, his choice of night-time timing and his provision of coordinates all show goal-directed decision-making, and his consideration of civilian risk shows that he appreciated the nature and consequences of the operation. This is consistent with Count 1: that Jaku endangered the Titans' mental health does not mean that it destroyed Thorn's capacity. The President's later remark that Thorn's plan ‘doesn't sound like you’ concerned a different operation, after the strike.¹³⁰ Thus, there are substantial grounds to believe that Thorn is individually criminally responsible under Article 25(3)(b) for the war crime under Article 8(2)(b)(ii).
