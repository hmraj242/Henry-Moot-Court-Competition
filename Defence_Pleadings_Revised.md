
### A. GENERAL THORN IS NOT INDIVIDUALLY CRIMINALLY RESPONSIBLE UNDER ARTICLE 25(3)(c) FOR THE WAR CRIME OF TORTURE OR INHUMAN TREATMENT, INCLUDING BIOLOGICAL EXPERIMENTS, UNDER ARTICLE 8(2)(a)(ii)

The Prosecution must support each charge with concrete and tangible proof establishing substantial grounds to believe that Thorn committed the crime charged.¹ The Defence will show that it has not done so for Count 1. First, Jaku was an emergency treatment, not a biological experiment; it did not seriously endanger the Titans' health; and it was therapeutic and in their interest. Secondly, the Titans were not protected persons for the purposes of Article 8(2)(a). Thirdly, no international armed conflict existed between 2 and 10 April 2025, and the treatment had no nexus to any conflict. Finally, Thorn did not aid, abet or assist any crime with the required purpose and knowledge.


#### 1. Jaku was an emergency treatment, not a biological experiment

A biological experiment is conduct whose primary purpose is to study the as yet unknown effects of a product on the human body.² The prohibition therefore turns on purpose and design, not on the novelty of the substance. It does not prevent doctors from trying new therapeutic methods dictated by the wish to improve the patient's condition,³ and the drafters intended precisely to preserve that freedom.⁴ If every first use of a new drug were an experiment, that exception would have no content. Purpose is assessed objectively, by asking whether the design and implementation of the programme are reasonable in relation to its stated objective,⁵ and a programme may pursue goals beyond its primary objective without losing its genuine character.⁶ The post-war cases show what a biological experiment looks like: prisoners in the enemy's power, deliberately infected to test vaccines, by doctors who took no risk themselves.⁷

Jaku bears none of these features. It was given to dying soldiers because conventional treatment had failed.⁸ Every patient received it, including the commanding officer, who was suffering from the same illness; Thorn was one more patient, not one more test subject.⁹ The dose was adjusted on the doctor's advice because many soldiers had not yet recovered, and the injections stopped as soon as all 40 had regained full health.¹⁰ There was no control group, no selection of subjects and no collection of data for study; the medical examinations recorded recovery, as every course of treatment does. Measured by the *Whaling* test, that design and implementation were reasonable in relation to one objective: restoring the soldiers' health. Dr Loff's further hopes for the drug, such as immunity, strength and national prestige, were secondary goals that did not change its primary purpose, and the description of Jaku as ‘ready for human trials’ concerned its stage of development, not the purpose for which it was given here.¹¹ As such, the Prosecution has offered no concrete and tangible proof that Jaku's primary purpose was to study its effects.


#### 2. The treatment did not seriously endanger the Titans' health

Whether an intervention seriously endangers health is judged by its foreseeable consequences ‘having regard to the state of health of the person’.¹² The standard is therefore relative: for a patient facing death, the relevant comparison is with the danger of withholding treatment. Serious harm to mental health requires more than passing symptoms. On the Prosecution's own authority, it must result in a grave and long-term disadvantage to a person's ability to lead a normal and constructive life.¹³ Inhuman treatment likewise requires severe physical or mental pain or suffering.¹⁴ The stricter regime of Article 11 of Additional Protocol I protects only persons ‘in the power of the adverse Party’ or otherwise deprived of liberty.¹⁵ Article 10(1) imposes a general duty to respect and protect all the wounded and sick; it does not extend the experiment regime of Article 11 to a Party's own forces, which the drafters deliberately confined to persons in the adversary's power.¹⁶

For soldiers in comas or ‘holding on for their lives’, the danger lay in the illness, not in the cure.¹⁷ Jaku woke the four comatose soldiers within three days and restored all 40 to full health within eight, with performance that ‘exceeded expectations’.¹⁸ Headaches subsided, and the remaining disorientation and paranoia were ‘sporadic’ and, on the medical advice received, ‘temporary’.¹⁹ Far from suffering a grave and long-term disadvantage, the Titans were ‘seemingly unaffected’ by the conditions that had defeated them before, and they went on to scale an escarpment undetected and destroy a radar installation.²⁰ The fatigue was reported three months after the charged period, and the July paranoia took the form of concern that the Titans' covert mission had been exposed.²¹ As such, the second element is not established.


#### 3. The treatment was therapeutic, medically justified and in the Titans' interest

*Salus aegroti suprema lex*: the welfare of the patient is the supreme law of medicine. Where proven treatments have been ineffective, a physician may, after seeking expert advice, use an unproven intervention that offers hope of ‘saving life, re-establishing health or alleviating suffering’.²² A measure that is a therapeutic necessity cannot, as a general rule, be regarded as inhuman or degrading, provided that the necessity is convincingly shown to exist.²³ A doctor who acts in accordance with a responsible and logically defensible body of medical opinion acts properly,²⁴ and the preservation of life in an emergency is the paramount duty of every doctor.²⁵

Consent confirms the therapeutic character. The Elements of Article 8(2)(a)(ii), unlike those of Article 8(2)(b)(x), do not exclude consent,²⁶ and that silence must be resolved in the accused's favour.²⁷ *Volenti non fit injuria*. Where a patient cannot consent in an emergency, legal systems across the world permit necessary treatment without consent: England on grounds of necessity and best interests,²⁸ Canada under the emergency doctrine,²⁹ India, where even the rule that consent to one procedure does not extend to another yields to the need to save life,³⁰ and the United States.³¹ Even where an intervention is classified as research, United States and European Union law permit it without prior consent in an emergency.³² That uniform practice is a general principle of law.³³ By contrast, *VC v Slovakia* concerned the sterilisation of a conscious and competent patient in no emergency at all.³⁴

Jaku met every part of this test. Regular treatment had failed, the soldiers were dying, and the head of Damaroi's leading biomedical institute advised that Jaku could restore their health, which it did.³⁵ The necessity was therefore convincingly shown by the record itself. The Prosecution's theory of enhancement does not withstand the facts. Thorn's remark that the Titans ‘need to be stronger’ was made before Jaku was ever proposed. The dose was raised ‘for optimal results’ because many soldiers still suffered symptoms, and the injections stopped once recovery was complete.³⁶ President Veyar's public description of the treatment as ‘for rehabilitation only’ is consistent with that record.³⁷

The conscious soldiers agreed to the treatment, and the four comatose soldiers could not consent and were in a life-threatening emergency.³⁸ For the conscious soldiers, adjusting the dose within a course of treatment they had already accepted required no fresh consent; for the comatose soldiers, the same emergency principle continued to apply.³⁹ The maxim *nemo dat quod non habet* is therefore beside the point: Thorn did not purport to supply a consent the soldiers had withheld, but authorised, as their commander, the continuation of a course of treatment on medical advice. In the further alternative, and without conceding that any element of the crime is established, Thorn's initial approval was necessary and reasonable to avoid the imminent threat of death to his soldiers, a threat beyond his control, and he intended no greater harm than the one he sought to avoid.⁴⁰ As such, the third element is not established.


#### 4. The Titans were not protected persons for the purposes of Article 8(2)(a)

Article 8(2)(a) criminalises grave breaches against persons ‘protected under the provisions of the relevant Geneva Convention’.⁴¹ The Elements identify those persons. The perpetrator ‘needs only to know that the victim belonged to an adverse party to the conflict’, and ‘[t]his footnote also applies to the corresponding element in each crime under article 8(2)(a)’.⁴² The footnote relaxes the knowledge required as to nationality precisely because protected status presupposes that the victim belongs to the adverse party; the drafters of every grave breach therefore assumed an enemy victim. The treaties must be read in that context and with the rest of the Geneva system,⁴³ which ties protected status to falling into the power of the other side.⁴⁴

The ICTY has consistently defined protected status by allegiance to the opposing party. *Tadić* and *Delalić* extended protection beyond formal nationality only to victims whose allegiance lay with the other side; neither extended it to a party's own forces.⁴⁵ Scholarship confirms that the law of armed conflict does not, as a rule, protect a party's own forces against their own State.⁴⁶ National courts agree: the Privy Council, on appeal from Malaysia, held that nationals of the detaining State could not claim prisoner-of-war protection against that State.⁴⁷ So does the first precedent on medical experiments: in the Medical Trial, experiments on German nationals were punished as crimes against humanity, and only those on prisoners of war and civilians of occupied territories were war crimes.⁴⁸

The Prosecution's reliance on the text of the Elements is misplaced. Articles 8(2)(b)(x)-2 and 8(2)(e)(xi)-2 needed an express requirement that the victim be in the power of an ‘adverse’ or ‘another’ party because, unlike Article 8(2)(a), they contain no protected-person element; in Article 8(2)(a), the same requirement is supplied by the protected-person element and footnote 33.⁴⁹ Nor does the *Ntaganda* appeal decision assist the Prosecution. It concerned rape and sexual slavery under Articles 8(2)(b)(xxii) and 8(2)(e)(vi), which carry no protected-status element, and it expressly contrasted them with the grave breaches of Article 8(2)(a), which do; its remarks on the First Geneva Convention were unnecessary to its decision.⁵⁰ *Nullum crimen sine lege stricta*: the definition of a crime ‘shall be strictly construed’ and, *in dubio pro reo*, any ambiguity resolved in favour of the accused.⁵¹

The Titans were Damaroi's own soldiers, treated by Damaroi's own medical service in a Damaran hospital.⁵² They had not fallen into the power of the adversary and owed allegiance to no party other than their own. Thorn knew that they belonged to his own side, not to an adverse party, so the awareness required by Element 5 is also absent.⁵³ As such, Elements 4 and 5 are not established.


#### 5. No international armed conflict existed between 2 and 10 April 2025, and the treatment had no nexus to any conflict

An IAC requires ‘armed hostilities between States through their respective armed forces’.⁵⁴ However low the threshold, some resort to armed force *against* another State is required,⁵⁵ and State practice supports a minimum level of hostilities.⁵⁶ International law has long distinguished genuine uses of armed force from ‘mere frontier incident[s]’. Although the International Court of Justice drew that distinction in the law of self-defence, it reflects the same requirement that force be directed against another State,⁵⁷ and *Armed Activities*, on which the Prosecution relies, concerned a large-scale military intervention and occupation.⁵⁸ Even where an IAC exists, the conduct must be associated with it: the conflict must have played a substantial part in the perpetrator's ability to commit the crime, his decision to commit it, the manner of its commission or its purpose,⁵⁹ and a relevant factor is whether the victim belonged to the opposing party.⁶⁰

Before 2 April, there had been no hostilities at all. The Titans engaged no Pallavon forces or civilians and turned back because of disease.⁶¹ The fire Thorn set on an uninhabited riverbank was treated by Pallavon itself as a natural event and never investigated.⁶² The five soldiers who continued into Pallavon never engaged any Pallavon force and, forensic evidence shows, died of the disease in early April; stranded soldiers dying of illness do not conduct hostilities.⁶³ Neither State acted as though it were at war. As late as 22 April, Pallavon's President declared that her country had ‘always maintained good relations with Damaroi’, and the first armed exchange occurred at Santo in June.⁶⁴

Nor did the treatment have any nexus to a conflict. The treatment of Damaroi's sick soldiers in a Damaran hospital by a Damaran doctor is exactly what would have occurred in peacetime, and the victims were not enemy nationals.⁶⁵ Thorn's remark that the Titans ‘need to be stronger’ was made before Jaku was ever proposed; it expressed general resolve, not a plan to experiment.⁶⁶ As such, Elements 6 and 7 are not established.


#### 6. Thorn did not aid, abet or otherwise assist the crime with the required purpose and knowledge

Accessory liability presupposes a principal crime.⁶⁷ Article 25(3)(c) further requires that the accessory act ‘for the purpose of facilitating’ the crime, a subjective threshold that goes beyond the knowledge required by Article 30.⁶⁸ The ad hoc tribunals' decisions on ‘specific direction’ applied a customary standard of knowledge, not the statutory requirement of purpose that the drafters deliberately chose.⁶⁹ The phrase derives from national law, where the accomplice must ‘in some sort associate himself with the venture’ and have advance knowledge of the circumstances that make the conduct criminal.⁷⁰ Even moral support must have a substantial effect on the crime and be given with knowledge that it assists the crime.⁷¹ The accessory must also know of every material element, including the non-therapeutic character of the experiment;⁷² recklessness and *dolus eventualis* do not suffice.⁷³ Milch was acquitted because the evidence did not show that he knew of the experiments' criminal character,⁷⁴ and an honest mistake of fact negates the mental element.⁷⁵

The principal, Dr Loff, administered Jaku believing that it ‘could help restore their health’.⁷⁶ Thorn's purpose was to save his soldiers, not to facilitate an experiment. His words show deference to medical judgement: ‘if the Doc says it's good to go, then it can't be wrong … if you think it'll help, I say we give it to the whole group’. The drug was obtained, prescribed and administered by others.⁷⁷ Thorn relied on an expert's assurance that the treatment would help and that its side-effects were temporary, and he took the drug himself while ill; no one who believed it to be a dangerous experiment would have injected himself.⁷⁸ Any error was one of fact, and it negates the mental element. *Actus non facit reum nisi mens sit rea*. Pre-Trial Chambers have declined to confirm charges where the Prosecution's proof fell short of this standard,⁷⁹ and Count 1 should not be confirmed.


### B. GENERAL THORN IS NOT INDIVIDUALLY CRIMINALLY RESPONSIBLE UNDER ARTICLE 25(3)(b) FOR THE WAR CRIME OF INTENTIONALLY DIRECTING ATTACKS AGAINST CIVILIAN OBJECTS UNDER ARTICLE 8(2)(b)(ii)

The Defence does not contest the contextual elements of Count 2, but the Prosecution has not established the remaining elements.⁸⁰ First, the three schools were military objectives by purpose, and Article 52(3) of AP I does not displace that purpose. Secondly, Thorn did not intend to attack civilian objects, and any shortfall in verification concerns precautions, not the crime charged. Finally, Thorn did not order, solicit or induce the attack.


#### 1. The contextual elements do not determine Count 2

Elements 4 and 5 require that the conduct took place in the context of and was associated with an international armed conflict, and that the perpetrator was aware of the factual circumstances establishing it.⁸¹ For present purposes, the Defence does not contest that the strike of 22 July 2025 satisfied those elements. They do not, however, establish the crime charged. The Prosecution must still prove that the three schools were civilian objects, that Thorn intended civilian objects to be the object of the attack, and that he ordered, solicited or induced it.⁸²


#### 2. The three schools were military objectives by purpose

Under Article 52(2) of AP I, an object is a military objective if, by its nature, location, purpose or use, it makes an effective contribution to military action and its destruction offers a definite military advantage in the circumstances ruling at the time. Both requirements are cumulative, and the definition is customary.⁸³ A civilian object ceases to be civilian once, by its location, purpose or use, it becomes a military objective; status changes with the circumstances.⁸⁴ *Ut res magis valeat quam pereat*: ‘purpose’ must be given its own meaning, for if it merely repeated ‘use’ it would be surplusage.⁸⁵ Purpose concerns the intended future use of an object, while use concerns its present function, and purpose must rest on concrete indicators rather than mere suspicion.⁸⁶

In *Hirgigo*, the Eritrea–Ethiopia Claims Commission agreed that purpose ‘means the future intended use of an object’. It held that a power station not yet producing power made an effective contribution to military action as the ‘intended replacement’ for capacity already serving military objectives, and it treated the adversary's own conduct as evidence of the object's military significance.⁸⁷ The Defence relies on *Hirgigo* only for the limited proposition that current operation is not necessary where an intended military purpose is concrete. Even President van Houtte, who dissented on the facts, accepted that an object which ‘made or could make’ an effective contribution may qualify, provided the basis for that conclusion is sufficiently specified.⁸⁸ The military advantage must be concrete and perceptible, and it is assessed in the context of the military operations as a whole.⁸⁹ A defending State must avoid locating military objectives within or near densely populated areas.⁹⁰ The Defence does not invoke military necessity as an independent justification; it relies on the definition in Article 52(2) itself.⁹¹

The schools' military purpose was concrete, specific and objectively established. First, Pallavon's Cabinet formally decided to repurpose every school larger than two hectares and within one kilometre of the railway ‘as new sites to train recruits and prepare operations’, in order to use their grounds, classrooms and access to the railway ‘to receive military equipment’.⁹² Those criteria identified exactly 20 schools, including the three attacked; the designation was therefore specific to the objects, not a general prediction. Secondly, the decision was implemented. Twelve of the 20 schools were initially converted into military facilities, and after attacks on them, five further qualifying schools were suspended, repurposed and struck after the Titans confirmed their conversion, each destroyed facility being replaced by the next qualifying school.⁹³ Thirdly, the final three were dismissed from teaching and their grounds evacuated, the same first step that had preceded every earlier conversion.⁹⁴ Fourthly, Pallavon was mobilising over two million recruits, receiving weapons and trainers by rail, and converting civilian buildings, including town halls, into training camps.⁹⁵ Far from offering alternatives, the town halls confirm Pallavon's systematic conversion of civilian buildings to military use.

Like the power station in *Hirgigo*, the schools were the intended replacement for capacity already lost, and Pallavon's own Cabinet had attributed military significance to them. These facts supply precisely the specification that President van Houtte found lacking. The Prosecution's comparison with hospitals or stadiums near a railway fails, because no Cabinet had decided to convert those objects. Their destruction also offered a definite military advantage: it denied Pallavon the capacity to reconstitute its training infrastructure in the middle of a mass mobilisation, a capacity it had exercised five times in a single week.⁹⁶ As such, the three schools were military objectives by purpose.


#### 3. Article 52(3) of AP I does not displace the evidence of military purpose

The presumption in Article 52(3) of AP I applies where there is doubt whether a school ‘*is being used*’ to make an effective contribution to military action.⁹⁷ *Expressio unius est exclusio alterius*: the provision names present use and says nothing of purpose. Reading it to cancel the separate purpose limb of Article 52(2) would deprive that limb of effect.⁹⁸ The Rome Statute itself protects buildings dedicated to education only ‘provided they are not military objectives’.⁹⁹ The Safe Schools Guidelines do not alter this. They are expressly non-binding and without prejudice to existing international law, and endorsement of the Safe Schools Declaration is a political commitment; neither can amend the definition of a military objective agreed by States in Article 52(2) and recognised as customary.¹⁰⁰

The Defence accepts that schools are ordinarily civilian and that the presumption governs doubt about present use. But President Veyar's doubt concerned only whether the schools were ‘actually being used’; Thorn's answer addressed their purpose: ‘Even if they are not being used now, their purpose is clear’.¹⁰¹ On purpose, the Cabinet decision, the conversion cycle and the evacuations left no genuine doubt. Nothing in the record shows that either State endorsed the Safe Schools Declaration, and Thorn's reasoning was not that necessity overrides the law, but that the schools met the definition of a military objective. That is the opposite of *Kriegsraison*. As such, Article 52(3) does not displace the schools' military purpose.


#### 4. Thorn did not intend to attack civilian objects

Element 3 requires that the perpetrator ‘intended such *civilian objects* to be the object of the attack’.¹⁰² The question is therefore not whether Thorn meant the missiles to strike the schools, but whether he meant to attack them as civilian objects. During the negotiation of the Elements, States rejected recklessness for this element: the perpetrator must mean the objects to be civilian targets or be aware that they are civilian in the ordinary course of events.¹⁰³ That standard means virtual certainty, not a possibility or a risk;¹⁰⁴ recklessness and *dolus eventualis* are excluded.¹⁰⁵ War crimes require knowledge of the facts that make the conduct criminal, and military personnel who, acting in good faith on erroneous information, attack an object they believe to be a military objective commit no crime.¹⁰⁶

Targeting decisions are judged on the information reasonably available at the time, not with hindsight.¹⁰⁷ That standard, first applied in *Rendulic*,¹⁰⁸ has passed into State practice,¹⁰⁹ and findings of unlawful attack cannot rest on standards constructed after the event.¹¹⁰ In the Kunduz airstrike, the German Federal Prosecutor's finding that a commander who acted on intelligence he believed reliable lacked criminal intent was held to rest on an adequate investigation.¹¹¹ A mistake of fact negates the mental element; even a mistake about a normative element of the crime may do so.¹¹²

Thorn acted on the Cabinet programme, the 12 initial conversions, the five confirmed replacements, the replacement pattern and the evacuations when he concluded that ‘every school of that size is being converted’.¹¹³ His departure from the earlier practice of confirmation does not show intent. Confirmation had concerned schools whose conversion was already under way; for the final three, the question was their purpose, and waiting for conversion would have meant striking them once they were filled with recruits. He recommended a night strike ‘to minimise risk of harming civilians’, the very precaution that IHL requires in attacks on military objectives.¹¹⁴ A commander who believes a site is about to become a military training facility is not virtually certain that he is attacking a civilian object. At most, Thorn was mistaken about whether Pallavon would imminently convert these particular schools. *Ignorantia facti excusat*: whether that mistake is characterised as factual or mixed, it negates the mental element, and in the alternative it is a mistake about the normative element ‘military objective’ that equally negates intent. If the Prosecution's real complaint is the civilian harm at the third school, that harm arose from an attack on what Thorn believed to be a military objective. It could at most raise a question of excessive incidental harm under Article 8(2)(b)(iv), a crime the Prosecution has not charged.¹¹⁵ As such, Element 3 is not established.


#### 5. Any shortfall in verification is a question of precautions, not the crime charged

Article 8(2)(b)(ii) punishes intentionally directing an attack against civilian objects; it does not criminalise a failure to verify a target. The duty to verify arises under Additional Protocol I,¹¹⁶ but the Rome Statute contains no corresponding war crime, and even Additional Protocol I treats as grave breaches only the wilful attacks it lists, not omissions in precaution.¹¹⁷ *Nullum crimen sine lege*: a duty of conduct cannot be turned into a crime of intentional attack by relabelling it.¹¹⁸ At most, a claim that Thorn should have waited longer alleges negligence, and negligence is not intent.¹¹⁹ In any event, the duty is to do everything *feasible* to verify, meaning what is practicable or practically possible in the circumstances ruling at the time, taking account of humanitarian and military considerations.¹²⁰ *Lex non cogit ad impossibilia*: the law does not demand certainty, but a reasonable judgement on the information available.¹²¹

Thorn met that standard. The Titans had monitored the converted schools across the country, supplying over 300 images and videos, delivery logs of weapons and uniforms, and records of night-time activity.¹²² They had observed that every conversion began with the school's suspension and evacuation, and that step had now occurred at all three schools.¹²³ The President raised his doubt and Thorn answered it on that evidence; a commander who weighs a doubt and resolves it on the information before him is verifying, not ignoring, the question.¹²⁴ Thorn proposed striking evacuated premises at night, the course least likely to harm anyone.¹²⁵ As such, any shortfall in verification is a precaution question, not the crime charged.


#### 6. Thorn did not order, solicit or induce the attack

Ordering requires a person in a position of authority who uses that authority to instruct another to commit the crime.¹²⁶ A formal relationship is not required, but authority over the person who carries out the crime must at least be reasonably implied from the evidence,¹²⁷ and the order must have a direct effect on the commission of the crime.¹²⁸ Ordering is not advice; it means using a subordinate to carry out the crime.¹²⁹ The label a narrative gives to a communication is not a finding that these elements are met,¹³⁰ and Pre-Trial Chambers have declined to confirm modes of liability unsupported by the evidence.¹³¹ Soliciting and inducing likewise require influence that has a direct effect on the commission of the crime, or that is a substantially contributing factor to it.¹³² *Affirmanti incumbit probatio*.

The agreed facts' use of the word ‘order’ does not settle the question, because the Prosecution identifies no recipient. The record does not show who launched the missiles, who authorised the launch, what chain of command linked Thorn to the missile force in Como City, or that anyone there was obliged to act on his word.¹³³ It shows instead that Thorn's formal role was to supply intelligence and planning. The President tasked him to be Damaroi's ‘eyes and ears’ and to supply ‘sound intel and a plan’; his words ‘I'll leave it with you’ entrusted Thorn with the intelligence and the plan, not with command of the missile campaign the President himself directed.¹³⁴ Thorn's earlier contribution took the form of a ‘recommendation’,¹³⁵ and the President's question, ‘Are you sure we should strike them?’, confirms that Thorn's word was not self-executing and that the decision to launch remained with the President.¹³⁶ Supplying coordinates is, at most, assistance, a mode not charged; it is not an order.

Nor did Thorn solicit or induce the attack. The President was already conducting a missile campaign to sting Pallavon ‘where it hurts’ and had his own reasons to continue it.¹³⁷ The record does not show that Thorn's assessment, rather than the President's independent decision, caused the launch, or that Thorn influenced any person who carried it out. As such, the Prosecution has not provided concrete and tangible proof of the mode of liability charged, and Count 2 should not be confirmed.
